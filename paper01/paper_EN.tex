\begin{abstract}
Integrated scheduling of processing and transport in flexible job shops usually treats travel times as a constant matrix indexed by location, i.e.\ it assumes that vehicles do not interfere with one another.
When a finite fleet shares a sparse corridor network, travel time is no longer fixed data but the outcome of routing decisions (in this paper, routing means conflict-free path planning of vehicles on the corridor network); one job's occupancy of a corridor affects the operation times of other jobs.
We study the following problem: in shops where transport takes longer than processing and congestion concentrates on a few shared corridors, each operation can be processed by one of several robotic arms that are homogeneous in capability but heterogeneous in efficiency, while AGVs must travel conflict-free on this network.
Machine flexibility turns this coupling into an operational trade-off: when the corridor to a fast arm is congested, a slower arm with better connectivity may be the better assignment. Prior work has not measured this trade-off by including it in the evaluation objective of the search.

We propose a \emph{closed-loop bilevel framework}: the upper level decides machine assignment and operation sequencing, and the lower level generates conflict-free paths by time-window reservation; routing information from the lower level enters the decisions in two ways.
The first is \emph{routing-in-the-loop evaluation} (the evaluation path): the fitness of each candidate solution is the makespan after conflict-free lower-level routing, so yielding delay enters the optimization objective of the search instead of serving only as a post-hoc check.
The second is \emph{reservation-table-aware dispatching} (the decision path): when a vehicle is selected, a trial query of both transport legs is run on the current reservation table, and the vehicle with the earliest actual delivery is chosen, instead of selecting a vehicle by travel times estimated under the assumption of an unimpeded trip.
To keep each dispatching evaluation \emph{low-overhead}, we give two speed-ups---admissible lower-bound pruning and winner-path reuse---and prove that they only reduce computation and change neither the selected vehicle nor the final timetable.

Experiments are run on \NCases{} controlled instances (contention intensity from \ContentionLo{} to \ContentionHi{}), with \NSeeds{} random seeds per instance, an equal wall-clock budget, and paired tests with multiple-comparison correction.
Starting from the open-loop structure in the literature (the search ignores conflicts, and vehicles are dispatched by unimpeded travel time), the complete method reduces the realized makespan by \GainEndToEnd{} (\WinEndToEnd{} wins, \LoseEndToEnd{} losses).
Most of the reduction comes from the closed loop: with the dispatching rule fixed and only the search made to count conflict-induced delay, makespan already falls by \GainLoopRule{}, and the reduction is significant per instance on the vast majority of instances; reservation-table-aware dispatching is a much smaller effect, significant only when all instances are pooled in one test. The two mechanisms each work on their own and do not amplify each other.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:Chapter_I}

\subsection{Background and motivation}

Flexible job shop scheduling with transportation times has long rested on a convenient assumption: the time a job needs to move between two machines is a constant, read from a matrix indexed by location pairs.
This is the dominant convention in the literature~\cite{xin2025review}, and recent models still widely adopt it~\cite{meng2025cp,qin2026knowledge,fu2025integrated}.
The assumption is reasonable when the fleet is small enough relative to the corridor network that vehicles rarely meet.
Real shops, however, are not built that way: a few vehicles share a sparse corridor network, and a single-lane segment cannot be traversed in both directions at the same time; once fleet density is high enough, the fleet's own traffic turns from a secondary disturbance into the main cause of delay~\cite{kim2026congestion}.
Once this is acknowledged, travel time is no longer fixed data but the outcome of two decisions: the path a vehicle takes (we call this conflict-free path planning routing), and the order of reservations on contended segments.
The duration of a transport therefore has two parts: the travel time computed from distance, and the time spent waiting at berths or detouring because corridors are occupied by other vehicles. The latter means that one job's transport can delay the operations of other jobs.

Such delay, passed from job to job, leaves room for optimization only if the scheduler can adjust the assignment of operations, and machine flexibility is exactly what allows an operation to be reassigned to another arm (i.e.\ to change its processing location).
When an operation may be processed by any robotic arm in an option set, and the processing time depends on the chosen arm, the assignment decision selects both the destination and the processing duration.
Suppose an operation may be given either to a fast arm at the far end of the shop or to a slower arm right next to the load/unload point.
Under a constant travel-time matrix the comparison is arithmetic: a shorter processing time plus a longer travel time, against a longer processing time plus a shorter travel time.
With corridor contention it is different, because the corridor to the fast arm may be exactly the one that every other vehicle is using, and the waiting incurred there is invisible to the constant matrix.
A slower arm with better connectivity may then be the better choice; and at different congestion levels of the same instance, the conclusion may differ again.
This trade-off requires two ingredients at once: heterogeneous optional arms give the scheduler destinations it can switch between, and distinct paths in the corridor network make vehicles wait and detour differently on the way.

Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} uses a recomputable minimal instance to show that the same pair of assignments favours the fast arm when evaluated with a constant travel-time matrix, and the slow arm when evaluated with conflict-free routing.
The instance has $4$ jobs and $2$ AGVs: one operation of the focal job J1 may use M1 ($t^P=3$) or M2 ($t^P=14$); the other three background jobs can be processed only on M3; finished jobs must be returned to the load/unload point $v_0$.
The only route to the fast arm M1 is an exclusive trunk corridor, while the slow arm M2 lies on a separate branch; the background jobs cross the trunk repeatedly and keep it occupied.

The numbers in Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} are of two kinds: (a) the bottom gives the round-trip time of the focal job J1 on its own, used to determine the minimum difference in processing time between the fast and the slow arm; (b) the bar heights are the instance makespans when all four jobs are scheduled together, used to compare the assignment rankings produced by the two evaluation methods.
The bottom of Figure~\ref{fig:motivating}(a) gives J1's unimpeded round-trip time (outbound + processing + return): J1 leaves $v_0$ and is returned to $v_0$ after processing, with travel times taken from shortest paths and no other vehicles assumed on the way.
When J1 is assigned to M1, the path is $v_0\to v_1\to v_2\to\mathrm{M1}$, one way $1+\tau+1=8$, round trip $16$, plus processing $3$, giving $19$;
when J1 is assigned to M2, the path is $v_0\to v_1\to\mathrm{M2}$, one way $1+2=3$, round trip $6$, plus processing $14$, giving $20$.
Ignoring the other vehicles, the fast arm is better if and only if $19<6+t^P_{\text{slow}}$, i.e.\ $t^P_{\text{slow}}>13$; with $\tau=6$ and $t^P_{\text{fast}}=3$, this instance takes $t^P_{\text{slow}}=14$, so the two assignments differ by only $1$ and the fast arm wins by the smallest possible margin.
Only because the fast arm is better under unimpeded conditions does the choice of the slow arm under conflict-free routing, shown below, constitute a ranking reversal.

The bar heights in Figure~\ref{fig:motivating}(b) are the instance makespan $C_{\max}$, i.e.\ the time at which the last finished job returns to $v_0$, obtained by the same decoder as in the rest of the paper.
All four jobs start from $v_0$ and are returned to it; J2, J3, and J4 can be processed only on M3 (outbound and return legs $8$ each, processing $3$ each); the two AGVs can operate in parallel.

Under the constant matrix, vehicles do not interfere with one another, and yielding in corridors does not enter the evaluation.
If J1 is assigned to M1, then $C_{\max}=41$: in the first wave, J1$\to$M1 and J2$\to$M3 run in parallel, each with outbound $8$, processing $3$, and return $8$, and both are back at $v_0$ at $t=19$;
in the second wave, J3 and J4 are both transported to M3; they leave together at $t=19$ and arrive at $t=27$ after outbound $8$; J3 is processed during $27$--$30$ and returns at $38$, while J4 waits until M3 is free at $30$, is processed until $33$, and returns after another $8$, so the latest return time is $41$.
If J1 is assigned to M2, then $C_{\max}=54$: J1 has outbound $3$, processing $14$, and return $3$; AGV1 waits at M2 until $t=17$ and is back at $v_0$ at $t=20$; J2 still returns at $19$.
From then on the operations of the two vehicles are no longer synchronized; J4 arrives at M3 at $43$ and returns to $v_0$ at $54$. The matrix evaluation still chooses the fast arm.

Now the same pair of assignments is evaluated by conflict-free routing.
With J1 assigned to M1, $C_{\max}=95$: J1's own unimpeded round-trip time is still $19$, but its outbound leg occupies the trunk during $[1,7)$ and its return leg during $[12,18)$; J2 yields $17$ at the trunk entrance and arrives at M3 at $25$; J2--J4 then pass through the trunk one after another, and J4 returns to $v_0$ at $95$.
With J1 assigned to M2, $C_{\max}=60$: J1 takes the branch and does not occupy the trunk, and J2 yields only $1$ on $v_0$--$v_1$; the background jobs still yield to one another, and J4 returns to $v_0$ at $60$. The slow arm is now better.
Neither the machine assignment nor the operation sequence has changed; only the evaluation method has.

\subsection{Gap relative to prior work}

A setting close to real AGV shops requires three conditions to hold at once: operations have optional machines, with processing times that depend on the machine; vehicles travel on an explicit road network with exclusive corridors, and travel times are determined jointly by the paths of all vehicles rather than taken from a constant matrix; and vehicles do not conflict with one another.
Most existing work covers only part of these: flexible job shop scheduling with transportation times represents travel by a constant travel-time matrix, and vehicles do not interfere with one another; conflict-free path planning handles vehicle travel, but the origins, destinations, and release times of tasks are given externally and do not change with the schedule.
Few works satisfy all three.
Van Os and Basten~\cite{vanos2026exact} formalize the problem as flexible job shop scheduling with conflict-free transportation constraints, and solve small benchmark instances to proven optimality with logic-based Benders decomposition.
This paper also studies flexible job shop scheduling with conflict-free routing, but focuses on a class of settings that prior work has not taken as a dedicated object of study: transport takes longer than processing ($\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p\approx4.0$, whereas common public instances reach at most \PubTtTpHi{}), robotic arms are connected by single-lane corridors, each corridor is exclusive over its whole length (van Os and Basten reserve nodes; see Section~\ref{sec:fjsppt}), vehicles may wait only at berths, and congestion concentrates on a few shared corridors.
In such shops, vehicle contention becomes the main constraint, and the quality of the same assignment changes with the level of congestion; we study this trade-off with contention intensity as a controlled variable, and target sizes that exact decomposition has difficulty solving.
At these sizes, existing heuristics either schedule first and resolve conflicts afterwards (Sun et al.~\cite{sun2023integrated}), or feed back the transport durations realized by the path layer once without modifying the assignment (Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework}), or embed routing in decoding without measuring its benefit and cost (Lyu et al.~\cite{lyu2019approach}).
Therefore, in this setting, it has not yet been measured under equal compute how the assignment should trade off between ``fast but congested'' and ``slow but clear'' arms, how much gain is obtained by counting yielding delay in the evaluation of the search, and at what level of contention this gain is worth its computational cost.

\subsection{Approach}

To address this gap, we adopt a bilevel structure: the upper level decides machine assignment and operation sequencing, and the lower level generates conflict-free paths by time-window reservation; routing information from the lower level enters the schedule in two ways, through evaluation and through decisions (detailed in the next two paragraphs), and we measure the contribution of each to makespan separately.

The \emph{evaluation path} takes the fitness of each candidate solution as the makespan after the lower level has completed conflict-free routing, so yielding wait enters the objective that the search optimizes, rather than serving as a post-hoc feasibility check.
One conflict-free decode costs more than an order of magnitude more computation than a decode based on unimpeded shortest-path times that ignore contention; under fixed compute, a higher cost per evaluation reduces the number of evaluations that can be completed.
Lyu et al.~\cite{lyu2019approach} already embed time-window routing in the decoding of a genetic algorithm, and Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework} list ``extending this into a fully iterative loop'' as future work, but neither reports the computational cost of this practice or its gain relative to open-loop scheduling. We measure this trade-off under an equal wall-clock budget.

The \emph{decision path} uses \emph{reservation-table-aware dispatching}. A transport consists of two legs: empty pickup and loaded delivery.
For each candidate vehicle, the lower-level router runs a trial query on the current reservation table for the earliest feasible times of the two legs in turn (the trial query does not write to the final timetable),
and the vehicle with the earliest feasible delivery is selected, rather than the vehicle whose arrival is earliest by an estimate based on unimpeded shortest-path times. This rule replaces the commonly used idle-first dispatching (a typical single-attribute rule in multi-AGV dispatching~\cite{lu2022agv}, also used by Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework}). To keep each evaluation \emph{low-overhead} under an equal wall-clock budget, we give two speed-ups:
admissible lower-bound pruning and winner-path reuse. Both skip only routing trial queries that cannot affect the result, and we prove that the selected vehicle and the timetable are exactly the same as without the speed-ups.


\subsection{Main results}

To measure the contributions of closed-loop evaluation and of reservation-table-aware dispatching separately, we construct a four-level baseline ladder whose first two levels represent two common process structures in the literature: \textbf{B0} is the open-loop structure, which first schedules by unimpeded travel times and then hands the schedule to conflict-free routing for execution (methods that schedule on a constant matrix~\cite{meng2025cp,qin2026knowledge,fu2025integrated} take exactly this structure when executed on a real road network, and the framework of Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework} also belongs to this class); \textbf{B0+} is the two-stage structure, which freezes the schedule and selects vehicles by the reservation table only in the second stage (e.g.\ Sun et al.~\cite{sun2023integrated});
\textbf{B1} is closed-loop planning with rule-based dispatching; \textbf{B2} is our method. The four configurations form a $2\times2$ design along two factors: whether the search counts conflict-induced delay, and whether vehicles are selected by unimpeded time or by trial queries on the reservation table.
Only a comparison that changes a single factor can attribute a gain to the corresponding mechanism.
The four configurations share the same upper-level search, the same router, and the same wall-clock budget, and differ only in the two places above; the differences therefore reflect only the structure, not how strong the implementation of each published algorithm is.
The makespan that an open-loop plan reports for itself is often infeasible on the actual corridors, so all schedules are executed by the same conflict-free router, and all comparisons use the realized makespan after execution.

The results show significant gains along both factors. With the dispatching rule fixed, including yielding wait in the fitness reduces makespan by \GainLoopRule{} ($p\PLoopRule$); this is the contribution of closed-loop evaluation;
within the closed loop, replacing rule-based dispatching with reservation-table-aware dispatching reduces makespan by \GainProbeLoop{} ($p\PProbeLoop$) relative to closed-loop planning with rule-based dispatching; this is the contribution of the dispatching mechanism;
from the open-loop structure to the complete method, the end-to-end reduction is \GainEndToEnd{} (\WinEndToEnd{} wins, \LoseEndToEnd{} losses, $p\PEndToEnd$). The three comparisons use different baselines and cannot be added directly.
The two low-overhead speed-ups make each dispatching evaluation \SpeedupProbe{} times faster and cut router calls by \RouteCallCut{}, lowering the cost multiple from \CostMultBefore{} to \CostMultAfter{}; this is the precondition for dispatching to yield a net gain under an equal wall-clock budget.

Both mechanisms are significant, but they are \emph{not substitutes for each other}: reservation-table-aware dispatching under the open loop yields \GainProbeOpen{} ($p\PProbeOpen$), far less than the closed-loop \GainLoopRule{}; the end-to-end \GainEndToEnd{} is slightly below the value \GainIndepProduct{} predicted by independent composition of the two, which shows that the two mechanisms each work on their own and do not amplify each other.
If only a single contrast from the open loop to the complete method were reported, the few percentage points from dispatching would be merged with the closed-loop gain into one term; we therefore report the two factors separately.

Results grouped by layout, vehicle-to-arm ratio, and flexibility are given in Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion}.
Per-instance tests are Holm-corrected by family.
After correction, the closed-loop gain remains significant on \CellsSigLoopAdj{} of the \NCases{} instances and rises roughly with contention intensity, so it can be interpreted along the layout axis; reservation-table-aware dispatching is significant on none of the instances (\CellsSigProbe{} before correction), and across the four levels of the vehicle-to-arm ratio the sign of its gain varies and none reaches significance.
Dispatching is therefore significant only when all instances are pooled in one test; with \NSeeds{} seeds per instance, the sample is not sufficient to identify on which class of instances it is stably positive.

These conclusions rest on two comparison rules (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). First, the computational cost per evaluation can differ by about two orders of magnitude across methods; comparing at an equal number of generations would let the more expensive method use more compute, so we align wall-clock time.
Second, each contrast changes only one factor (whether the search counts delay, or how vehicles are selected); a contrast that changes both at once yields only a combined effect and cannot tell whether the gain comes from the closed loop or from dispatching.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{Instances: instance families with controllable contention intensity, for studying the reassignment trade-off between fast and slow arms.} When machine flexibility, heterogeneous processing efficiency, and corridor contention are all present, and the schedule targets the makespan with yielding wait counted, a slower arm with better connectivity may be the better assignment if the corridor to a fast arm is congested.
Models that schedule on a constant travel-time matrix cannot reflect this comparison, because the matrix contains no yielding wait~\cite{sun2023integrated};
models with conflict-free routing but a fixed machine for each operation do not involve this comparison either, because there is no reassignment~\cite{li2026framework}.
Van Os and Basten~\cite{vanos2026exact} and Lyu et al.~\cite{lyu2019approach} also consider flexible assignment under conflict-free routing, but do not study its effect with contention intensity as a controlled variable; targeting shops where transport takes longer than processing and congestion concentrates on shared corridors, we construct instance families with controllable contention intensity, to examine how high contention must be before counting yielding wait in the schedule brings a gain.

\item \textbf{Framework: a closed-loop bilevel framework, with a measurement of the contribution of routing-in-the-loop evaluation to makespan.} \emph{Routing-in-the-loop evaluation} takes the fitness of each candidate solution as the makespan after the lower level has completed conflict-free routing, so yielding wait enters the optimization objective of the search.
We measure the gain and the computational cost of this practice relative to the open loop (the search ignores conflicts) under an equal wall-clock budget, and prove that every chromosome can be decoded into a conflict-free, deadlock-free timetable, so the search needs no repair operator.

\item \textbf{Mechanism: reservation-table-aware dispatching, and two speed-ups that keep it \emph{low-overhead}.} When a vehicle is selected, a trial query of both transport legs is run on the current reservation table, and the vehicle with the earliest feasible delivery is chosen.
We give two speed-ups, admissible lower-bound pruning and winner-path reuse, and prove that they change neither the selected vehicle nor the final timetable; it is precisely this reduction in overhead that lets the mechanism achieve a net gain under an equal wall-clock budget.

\item \textbf{Evidence: the two paths contribute at different magnitudes, each works on its own, and they are not substitutes for each other.} The closed loop is the larger effect, and nearly all of its per-instance tests are significant; reservation-table-aware dispatching is a much smaller effect, significant only when all instances are pooled in one test; the two do not amplify each other, and changing only vehicle selection under the open loop is far from a substitute for counting yielding wait in the search.
Two further inter-layer interfaces (an extra cost on occupied corridor--time slots, hereafter surcharge, and reassignment or staggering guided by yield records) only add overhead without reducing makespan under equal compute; the failure of the surcharge indicates that, before extending the inter-layer interface, one should first check whether the objective of the receiving level already contains the quantity that the interface is meant to pass.


\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:Chapter_II}

Related work falls into three groups: flexible job shop scheduling with transportation, conflict-free routing and dispatching with exogenous tasks, and integrated work that brings the two into one problem. Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes how each group compares with this paper.

\subsection{Flexible job shop scheduling with transportation}

Flexible job shop scheduling with transportation (FJSP\_PT) brings material movement into the schedule. Xin et al.~\cite{xin2025review} review progress in the field through 2023 and point out that the dominant modelling convention is a constant travel-time matrix indexed by location pairs: a vehicle needs a fixed time $t(a,b)$ to travel from $a$ to $b$, which does not change with whether other vehicles occupy the same corridor. For modelling and algorithms of the general FJSP, see Dauz\`ere-P\'er\`es et al.~\cite{dauzereperes2024fjsp}; the public benchmarks of this family are collected by Homayouni and Fontes~\cite{homayouni2021production}.

% [ref_check: key=xin2025review; claim="常数行驶时间矩阵是 FJSP_PT 文献中占主导地位的建模约定"; need=quote]

Recent work keeps this matrix and advances mainly on solution methods. Meng et al.~\cite{meng2025cp} propose a constraint-programming model for the multi-AGV case and a dual-population genetic algorithm assisted by constraint programming, closing 29 open benchmark instances and improving 27 best-known solutions; Qin et al.~\cite{qin2026knowledge} combine a quality--diversity archive with Q-learning and design a local search jointly guided by AGVs and the critical path; Fu et al.~\cite{fu2025integrated} use an improved genetic algorithm to decide production and logistics jointly in a flexible assembly shop.
In all of these works, however, transport durations are taken from the matrix, so vehicles never delay one another through corridor contention; and although critical-chain reasoning is a mature device in job-shop local search~\cite{otala2021graph,tamssaouet2023neighborhood}, a critical path computed on the matrix can only be traced back to a predecessor operation or a busy machine, not to a specific corridor.
Xin et al.~\cite{xin2025review} also note that such assumptions ``reduce the complexity of the problem and even change its nature'', and that existing instances ``differ mainly in size and lack a dedicated design for the values of key problem parameters''; they list the ratio of mean transport time to mean processing time, $\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p$, as a quantity that is highly likely to affect algorithm performance.

\subsection{Conflict-free routing with exogenous tasks and congestion-aware dispatching}

Multi-agent path finding (MAPF) studies the collision-free movement of several agents on a shared graph, and solving it optimally is NP-hard~\cite{gao2023mapf}; Kasaura et al.~\cite{kasaura2022sipp} extend Safe Interval Path Planning to two-dimensional road networks and continuous time.
For settings with continuously arriving tasks, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024guidance} optimize offline a road network with edge weights that steers agents away from bottlenecks, raising the throughput of several solvers.
Kim and Kim~\cite{kim2026congestion} propose a congestion function, a congestion penalty, and congestion-aware dispatching rules for large AGV fleets.
Surveys of multi-AGV management and of mobile-robot control~\cite{lu2022agv,fragapane2021planning} treat dispatching and path planning as two adjacent but separate layers of the control stack, and list idle-first and nearest-vehicle as common single-attribute dispatching rules.
In this line of work, however, the release time, origin, and destination of a task are all inputs to the router, which cannot revise the machine assignment that generates these tasks; congestion is also mostly quantified by indirect measures such as local vehicle density or offline edge weights, which can only show that a corridor carries relatively many vehicles, not which transport caused yielding, when, and where.

\subsection{Integrating scheduling and conflict-free routing}

Work that considers flexible assignment and conflict-free routing together is scarce.
van Os and Basten~\cite{vanos2026exact} formalize FJSP with conflict-free transportation constraints and propose a logic-based Benders decomposition: a constraint-programming master problem gives the assignment and sequence, and an integer-programming subproblem plans conflict-free paths and returns conflicts to the master as cuts. Most benchmark instances are solved to proven optimality, and about half improve makespan by at least 10\% relative to prior heuristics. The exact decomposition, however, applies only to small instances; the authors also list making fuller use of ``which conflicts caused the infeasibility'' as a direction for future work.
Sun et al.~\cite{sun2023integrated} propose a two-stage method: a hybrid genetic algorithm first fixes the assignment and sequence, and time-window Dijkstra then resolves conflicts, with every node and every segment holding at most one vehicle at a time; but even when the second stage detects corridor congestion, it cannot change the choices made in the first.
Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework} forbid both node and edge conflicts on a grid and add one feedback step that writes the transport durations realized by the path layer back onto the time axis; but the feedback only shifts start times without revising the assignment, acts on the current solution only, and their benchmark has a single eligible machine per operation.
Lyu et al.~\cite{lyu2019approach} embed time-window Dijkstra in genetic-algorithm decoding, so that the makespan of every chromosome is computed after routing; but the research focus of that paper is determining the fleet size, its instances are small, it does not compare against open-loop scheduling, and it does not report the computational cost of the embedded routing.

In summary, the gap in existing work lies not in whether vehicle conflicts are handled, but in how routing results enter the schedule, and in what doing so is worth.

\section{Problem statement and model}
\label{sec:Chapter_III}
\subsection{Problem statement}

An instance consists of $n$ jobs, $N_M$ robotic arms, and $N_A$ vehicles (AGVs), operating on a shop-floor road network represented as a graph.
Job $j$ has a fixed chain of $n_j$ operations $O_{j1}\to\cdots\to O_{jn_j}$; operation $O_{ji}$
may be processed on any robotic arm in its option set $\Omega_{ji}\subseteq M$, taking time
$t^P_{jim}$ on arm $m$, a value that depends on the chosen arm. Robotic arms occupy fixed nodes of the graph, and jobs are moved between these nodes by the fleet.
Solving an instance means determining four groups of decisions at once:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
\item assign each operation $O_{ji}$ to some robotic arm $m\in\Omega_{ji}$;
\item sequence the operations assigned to each robotic arm;
\item assign the induced transport tasks to vehicles and sequence the tasks of each vehicle;
\item plan a path on the graph for every vehicle move, conflict-free in time and space, with waiting allowed.
\end{enumerate}

The objective is to minimize the makespan $C_{\max}$.

\subsection{Modelling assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{A\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Static instance and complete information.} All $n$ jobs are already at $v_0$ at time $t=0$; there are no release dates or due dates. Processing data, the layout $G$, and the vehicle states are all known at planning time.

\item \textbf{Jobs.} Each job has a fixed precedence chain. Processing and transport are non-preemptive, jobs cannot be split,
and there are no exogenous precedence constraints among different jobs.

\item \textbf{Robotic arms: partial flexibility and heterogeneous processing times.} Each operation $O_{ji}$ may be processed on any
$m\in\Omega_{ji}$; most operations satisfy $|\Omega_{ji}|\ge 2$, and full flexibility
$\Omega_{ji}=M$ is a special case. Processing times vary with the chosen arm, and the arms need not share a uniform ranking by speed: when
$m\neq m'$, $t^P_{jim}$ and $t^P_{jim'}$ need not be proportional. An arm processes only
one operation at a time and does not fail during the planning horizon; sequence-dependent setup times are folded into $t^P_{jim}$.

\item \textbf{Buffers and loading.} The input and output buffers of each arm, and the storage at $v_0$, have ample capacity,
so blocking does not occur. Loading and unloading times are negligible and do not occupy the arm.

\item \textbf{Vehicles.} The $N_A$ vehicles are homogeneous and unit-load, and travel at constant speed whether empty or loaded; acceleration, deceleration, and
battery charge are not modelled. All vehicles stand empty at $v_0$ at $t=0$ and are immediately available. A transport task is a one-way move---from $v_0$
to the first arm, between two successive distinct arms, or, when $\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}=1$, from the last arm
back to $v_0$; any vehicle may execute any task,
so successive moves of the same job may be relayed across different vehicles. After unloading, a vehicle is not forced back to $v_0$; it
repositions according to its own task chain, and its departure time may be postponed by the routing layer.

\item \textbf{Road network and conflict-freedom.} $G$ is strongly connected, and the traversal time $\tau_e$ of each corridor $e\in E$ is a known constant. Corridors are
\emph{exclusive} resources: the two directions of each corridor $e$ share a single reservation, so at most one vehicle occupies $e$ at any time, which rules out
both head-on conflicts and same-direction overtaking. Occupancy intervals are half-open, $[t,\,t+\tau_e)$, so a second vehicle may enter exactly at
$t+\tau_e$. Nodes have berths of ample capacity, independent of the traversal resources; waits therefore occur at berths
and are always permitted.

\item \textbf{Out of scope.} Machine and vehicle failures, finite buffers and blocking, battery charging, and dynamic
job arrivals~\cite{gui2023dynamic,xiong2024dynamic} are left to future work (item~(5) of Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).
\end{enumerate}

Of these, A1, A2, A4, and A5 are standard assumptions in the FJSP\_PT literature~\cite{xin2025review}. The features that define our problem are A3 (machine-dependent processing times on partially flexible robotic arms) and A6 (contention stated as an explicit exclusive-resource constraint rather than a constant travel-time matrix);
as a result, transport duration is an output of the routing decision, not a fixed input parameter.
Deadlock-freedom is not an assumption: Proposition~\ref{prop:decodable} in Section~\ref{sec:upper} uses A4 and A6 to prove that any assignment and operation sequence can be decoded into a conflict-free, deadlock-free timetable, so the search need not repair infeasible solutions.

\subsection{Decision variables and induced transport tasks}

Let $x_{jim}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate that $O_{ji}$ is assigned to robotic arm $m$; write $\mathrm{m}(j,i)$ for the chosen arm and
$d_{ji}=\mathrm{loc}(\mathrm{m}(j,i))$ for the node of that arm. When $\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}=1$,
a zero-duration \emph{dummy operation} $O_{j,n_j+1}$ at $v_0$ is appended to each job, representing the return to the load/unload point after completion;
in this way return moves and intermediate moves share the same task notation, and the makespan is always the completion time of each job's last operation.

Unlike in a vehicle routing problem, transport tasks are not part of the input; they are \emph{induced} by the assignment:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tasks}
\mathcal{T}(x)=\Bigl\{\,(j,i)\ :\ d_{j,i-1}\neq d_{ji}\,\Bigr\},
\qquad d_{j0}\equiv v_0 ,
\end{equation}
where task $(j,i)$ moves job $j$ from $d_{j,i-1}$ to $d_{ji}$ and becomes ready at time
$r_{ji}=C_{j,i-1}$ (with the convention $C_{j0}=0$). If two successive operations are placed on the same arm, no
transport task is created. Thus both the \emph{number} and the \emph{endpoints} of transport tasks are consequences of decision group~(i): an assignment that keeps a job
on the same arm throughout needs only two moves, whereas an assignment that changes arm at every operation needs $n_j+1$ moves.
Assignment of tasks to vehicles is indicated by $y_{tk}\in\{0,1\}$, with
$\sum_{k\in K}y_{tk}=1$ for each $t\in\mathcal{T}(x)$; tasks given to the same vehicle are ordered into a sequence.

In a constant travel-time matrix model, the assignment likewise determines the number of moves and their endpoints, but the duration of each move is a constant in the matrix, and corridor occupancy does not enter the model.
In this paper, the same assignment also determines on which corridors those moves contend with one another.

\subsection{Timing relations and feasibility}

Write $p_{ji}=\sum_{m\in\Omega_{ji}}x_{jim}t^P_{jim}$ for the processing time on the chosen arm. Assignment, precedence, and
the unit capacity of each arm give
\begin{align}
\sum_{m\in\Omega_{ji}} x_{jim} &= 1, & \forall j,i,
\label{eq:assign}\\
C_{ji} &= S_{ji}+p_{ji}, & \forall j,i,
\label{eq:dur}\\
S_{ji} &\ \ge\ A_{ji}, & \forall j,i,
\label{eq:material}\\
S_{j'i'}\ \ge\ C_{ji}\ \ \vee\ \ S_{ji} &\ \ge\ C_{j'i'}
& \forall (j,i)\neq(j',i')\ \text{on the same arm},
\label{eq:disj}
\end{align}
where $A_{ji}$ is the time at which the job actually appears at $d_{ji}$. If $(j,i)\notin\mathcal{T}(x)$, the job is
already there and $A_{ji}=C_{j,i-1}$. Otherwise $A_{ji}$ is produced by the vehicle that carries task $(j,i)$: write
$a^{\mathrm{E}}_{ji}$ for the time at which that vehicle reaches the pickup node after its empty leg; then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:load}
\ell_{ji}=\max\bigl(a^{\mathrm{E}}_{ji},\ r_{ji}\bigr),
\qquad
A_{ji}=\text{arrival time of the loaded leg departing at } \ell_{ji},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the vehicle may wait for the job, and the job may wait for the vehicle. Whether empty or loaded, each leg is a walk
$(e_1,\dots,e_q)$ in $G$ whose corridor entry times $\eta_1,\dots,\eta_q$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:walk}
\eta_1 \ \ge\ \sigma, \qquad
\eta_{l+1}\ \ge\ \eta_l+\tau_{e_l}, \quad l=1,\dots,q-1,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the time at which the leg may start.
If $\eta_{l+1}=\eta_l+\tau_{e_l}$, the vehicle enters the next corridor without stopping; if $\eta_{l+1}>\eta_l+\tau_{e_l}$, the difference is the vehicle's waiting time at a berth. The leg ends at $\eta_q+\tau_{e_q}$.
Finally, the corridor-contention constraint is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:excl}
\bigl[\eta,\ \eta+\tau_e\bigr)\ \cap\
\bigl[\eta',\ \eta'+\tau_e\bigr)\ =\ \emptyset
\end{equation}
This constraint holds for any two distinct occupancies of the same corridor $e$, regardless of direction and of which vehicles are involved; it is also the only constraint in the model that couples different jobs through \emph{space} rather than through a shared arm. The objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:obj}
\min\ C_{\max},\qquad
C_{\max}=\max_{j\in J}\ C_{j,\,n_j+\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}} .
\end{equation}

In a semi-active schedule (every operation starts at the earliest time the constraints allow), Eqs.~\eqref{eq:material} and \eqref{eq:disj} tighten to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bind}
S_{ji}=\max\bigl(A_{ji},\ F_{\mathrm{m}(j,i)}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $F_m$ is the completion time of the operation sequenced immediately before $O_{ji}$ on arm $m$; that is, the start time is the later of the job's arrival and the completion of the preceding operation on that arm. Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_IV} uses this to attribute a postponed start to waiting for the workpiece or waiting for the arm.

\subsection{Relation to FJSP\_PT}
\label{sec:fjsppt}

Once Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl} is removed, legs no longer occupy corridors against one another, so every leg can take a shortest path without waiting along the way. Transport durations collapse to the constant matrix $t^{*}(a,b)$,
and the model becomes flexible job shop scheduling with transportation (FJSP\_PT). The two problems therefore differ only in this set of corridor-exclusivity constraints: with them, the duration of a move is jointly determined by the vehicles' paths; without them, the duration is an input parameter.
The resulting $t^{*}$ is used later as a lower bound, and in the open-loop baseline to estimate arrival times by unimpeded travel time.

Relative to work that likewise supplies an explicit road network, the difference lies in what is reserved. van Os and Basten~\cite{vanos2026exact}
reserve \emph{nodes}: two vehicles may not occupy the same node at the same time, nor swap nodes. This paper reserves \emph{edges}: corridors are exclusive in both directions, and at most one vehicle occupies a corridor at any time.
Lyu et al.~\cite{lyu2019approach} likewise make corridors exclusive in both directions, but additionally allow each node to hold only one vehicle at a time; this paper instead provides berths of ample capacity at nodes for vehicles to wait in, consistent with how Assumption A4 treats arm buffers and the storage at $v_0$ and corresponding to the vehicle standby areas beside workstations in real shops, so congestion occurs only on corridors.
The reasons why the instances cannot be compared directly are given under limitation~(1) in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Computational complexity}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:np_hard}
The problem defined by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:assign}--\eqref{eq:obj} is strongly NP-hard, and it remains so when every operation satisfies
$\Omega_{ji}=M$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Restrict to instances in which $\tau_e=0$ for every $e\in E$, each robotic arm is connected to $v_0$ by a dedicated corridor,
and $N_A=n$. Every leg then takes zero time and occupies the empty half-open interval $[t,t)=\emptyset$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl}
holds trivially and no vehicle can delay another; moreover, because each job has a vehicle of its own, Eq.~\eqref{eq:load} never binds and
$A_{ji}=C_{j,i-1}$. What remains is exactly
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:assign}--\eqref{eq:disj} with $A_{ji}=C_{j,i-1}$, i.e.\ the flexible job shop scheduling problem with unrelated machines. Two of its
special cases are already strongly NP-hard: taking $|\Omega_{ji}|=1$ everywhere reduces it to the classical job shop problem, which is
strongly NP-hard already with three machines; taking $\Omega_{ji}=M$ and $n_j=1$ reduces it to makespan minimization on unrelated parallel
machines, which contains identical-parallel-machine scheduling and is strongly NP-hard when the number of machines is part of the input.
Flexible job shop scheduling in general is itself strongly NP-hard~\cite{dauzereperes2024fjsp}. Each of the restrictions above is a
polynomial reduction, so the general problem is strongly NP-hard; the second special case also shows that full flexibility does not make the problem easier.
\end{proof}

The reduction in Proposition~\ref{prop:np_hard} sets corridor traversal times to zero and thereby excludes vehicle contention. This shows that the problem is already strongly NP-hard even without corridor contention.
If decisions~(i)--(iii) are then fixed, the remaining path planning is still a conflict-free multi-agent routing problem~\cite{gao2023mapf}, in which the release times and endpoints of the tasks are determined by the operation chains and the assignment and constrain one another.
Conflict-free routing therefore adds constraints on top of the scheduling difficulty rather than replacing it. Exact methods already exist at small scale: van Os and Basten~\cite{vanos2026exact} use logic-based Benders decomposition
to solve most benchmark instances under the same problem setting to proven optimality. This paper targets sizes beyond the practical reach of that exact decomposition.

\subsection{Instance features}
\label{sec:features}

This paper holds instance size fixed and varies only structure; the effect of size itself is discussed under limitation~(1) in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
Each instance records the following features. The first four are quantities common in the scheduling literature; Xin et al.~\cite{xin2025review} note that $\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p$ among them is likely to affect algorithm performance.


\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
  \item $\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p$: the mean shortest-path travel time between arm nodes divided by the mean processing time, i.e.\ the ratio of transport time to processing time.
  \item Heterogeneity $H$: the coefficient of variation of each operation's processing times over its optional arms, averaged over all operations (operations with $|\Omega_{ji}|=1$ contribute $0$).
  $H=0$ means that the optional arms have identical processing times: reassignment does not affect processing time but can still be used to avoid congestion.
  As $H$ grows, processing-time differences increasingly dominate the assignment and the room for reassignment to avoid congestion shrinks accordingly, so we do not expect the gain to rise with $H$; the measurements grouped by $H$ in Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} agree with this.
  \item Flexibility $F=\operatorname{mean}_{ji}|\Omega_{ji}|/N_M$: the mean number of optional arms per operation as a fraction of all arms, i.e.\ the scope of reassignment.
  \item $N_A/N_M$: the vehicle-to-arm ratio. A smaller ratio makes the bottleneck more likely to lie in transport; a larger ratio, in processing.
\end{itemize}

We also record two quantities that are meaningful only on an explicit road network, describing the severity of congestion and the part of it that reassignment can avoid.
Let $\mathrm{cut}(v_0)$ be a smallest set of corridors whose removal disconnects the load/unload point $v_0$ from every robotic arm.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
  \item Contention intensity: on the initial random population, the share of total travel time that is yielding wait. It is determined by the instance structure rather than supplied as an input parameter; we use it to order the instances, and the per-instance values are given in Table~\ref{tab:instances}.
  \item $|\mathrm{cut}(v_0)|$ (funnel width): an upper bound on the number of vehicles that can leave the load/unload point at the same time. The value $1$ means that every vehicle must pass through the same corridor to leave. The layout family (Family~A) varies contention by adding or removing such parallel exits; relative to \texttt{high}, \texttt{funnel} halves this value.
\end{itemize}

Each instance carries a composite lower bound
$\mathrm{LB}=\max(\mathrm{LB}_{\text{chain}},\mathrm{LB}_{\text{load}},
\mathrm{LB}_{\text{cut}})$, computed directly from the instance data. The three components each relax a different part of the model, and none is always the largest on every instance.
$\mathrm{LB}_{\text{chain}}$ takes, for each job, ``the shortest inbound trip $+$
$\sum_i \min_{m\in\Omega_{ji}}t^P_{jim}$ $+$ the shortest return trip'', i.e.\ it ignores inter-arm moves and all queueing.
$\mathrm{LB}_{\text{load}}$ takes $\sum_{j,i}\min_m t^P_{jim}/N_M$, i.e.\ it ignores operation precedence and transport.
$\mathrm{LB}_{\text{cut}}$ exploits the funnel: when $\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}=1$ each job crosses
$\mathrm{cut}(v_0)$ twice; spreading the $X=2n$ crossings over the corridors of that cut so as to minimize the load on the busiest one yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lbcut}
C_{\max}\ \ge\ X\Bigl/\sum_{e\in \mathrm{cut}(v_0)}\tau_e^{-1}.
\end{equation}
On the instances of this paper, the largest of the three is $\mathrm{LB}_{\text{cut}}$ (on eight of the ten instances) or
$\mathrm{LB}_{\text{chain}}$ (on the other two), and $\mathrm{LB}_{\text{load}}$ is never the largest---which agrees with an instance design that concentrates contention at the load/unload exit.
The bound is fairly loose: its gap to the best known solution is roughly between \LBGapLo{} and \LBGapHi{}, so it is used only to check the order of magnitude, not to prove that a solution is near-optimal.

\section{Closed-loop bilevel framework}
\label{sec:Chapter_IV}
\subsection{Overview: routing-in-the-loop evaluation and the decision path}
\label{sec:overview}

The framework assigns the four groups of decisions of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_III} to two levels. The input is an instance: the operation chains of the jobs,
the option sets and processing times of the arms, the fleet size, and the corridor network. The upper level searches over assignment and sequencing with a memetic algorithm~\cite{zhang2024memetic}; in the middle,
an event-driven decoder translates one upper-level candidate into a sequence of precedence-constrained transport tasks; the lower level turns
this sequence into conflict-free paths by time-window reservation.
The output is an executable conflict-free timetable and its makespan.
Relative to two-stage schemes or schemes that correct only once, the difference lies not in having two levels but in how the routing result enters the schedule: the content and direction of the feedback, and whether every candidate receives it.


\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,topsep=3pt,itemsep=3pt]
  \item[The evaluation path, i.e.\ \emph{routing-in-the-loop evaluation}.]
  In every generation, the fitness of each candidate is the makespan after the lower level has completed conflict-free routing of all transport tasks, yielding wait included.
  The upper level takes this value as its objective, rather than the makespan computed on the assumption that vehicles never interfere and always travel unimpeded.
  \item[The decision path.]
  Vehicle selection reads the current reservation table, instead of learning the outcome only after the paths have been generated.
  The mechanism is \emph{reservation-table-aware dispatching}: for each candidate vehicle, the earliest feasible times of the empty leg and then the loaded leg are obtained by a trial query on the reservation table (the trial query does not write the final timetable), and the vehicle with the earliest feasible delivery is selected, rather than the vehicle whose arrival is earliest by the unimpeded-shortest-path estimate.
\end{description}

The contributions of the two paths to makespan are measured separately in Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V}.
The two further inter-layer interfaces implemented are an extra cost on occupied corridor--time slots (hereafter, surcharge) and reassignment or resequencing from the yield record; their constructions are given in Sections~\ref{sec:interface} and~\ref{sec:ls}, and the control results in Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V}.

The structure of the framework is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}. The two paths enter the upper level at different places, and the computational cost of a single evaluation therefore differs as well.

\subsection{Lower level: time-window reservation and conflict-free routing}
\label{sec:router}

\subsubsection{Reservation table}
The only contended resource is the corridor (assumption~A6), so the reservation table maps each corridor to a time-ordered list of half-open occupancy intervals $[t,\,t+\tau_e)$, each labelled with the vehicle and task that occupy it.
The table supports four operations: query the earliest feasible entry time no earlier than a given instant; write an occupancy; revoke every occupancy of a given task; and save the current table state and restore it after a trial query (checkpoint and rollback).
Nodes are not reserved: traversing a node takes zero time, and waits occur at berths (assumption~A6 already separates berths from the traversal resource).
Only one resource, the corridor, therefore needs to be reserved, and both the lower-level conflict-free path planner (hereafter, the router) and a checker independent of the decoder inspect only corridor occupancy.

\subsubsection{Time-window Dijkstra}
A leg is planned by a label-setting search. A label $(u,t)$ means ``standing at the berth of node $u$ at time $t$'', and its cost is $t$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:route}).
When a corridor leaving the current node is examined, the reservation table is queried for the earliest feasible entry time $t'\ge t$; the gap $t'-t$ is the time the vehicle waits at the berth of $u$.
The wait in Eq.~\eqref{eq:walk} is therefore produced directly by the search and need not be modelled separately.
Within one decoding, paths already written to the reservation table are not revoked, so vehicles are planned one after another in the fixed order in which the decoder generates tasks, and a later-planned vehicle yields to an earlier-planned one.

\begin{lem}[One earliest-arrival label per node suffices]
\label{lem:label}
Keeping only the earliest-arrival label at each node is sufficient to obtain a path that reaches the destination earliest under the current reservation table.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  Berths have ample capacity and do not occupy the traversal resource (A6), so a vehicle that has stood at $u$ since time $t$ need only wait further until $t'$
  in order to take any continuation that a vehicle arriving at $u$ only at $t'>t$ could take.
  An earlier-arrival label is therefore never worse.
  Moreover, the later the query time to the reservation table, the fewer (never more) the still-feasible entry slots,
  so the time to enter corridor $e$ and finish traversing it,
  $t\mapsto R.\textsc{EarliestEntry}(e,t,\tau_e)+\tau_e$,
  is non-decreasing in $t$.
  The search extracts labels in increasing order of arrival time, and the first time the destination is extracted is the earliest arrival.
\end{proof}

Ordinary Dijkstra therefore suffices, and there is no need for the per-interval labelling of Safe Interval Path Planning (SIPP)~\cite{kasaura2022sipp}; if a second criterion is introduced (for example, a surcharge on corridor--time slots), earlier arrival is no longer always better, and a multi-label search is required (Section~\ref{sec:interface}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{\textsc{Route}: conflict-free planning of a single leg}
  \label{alg:route}
  \small
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require origin $s$, destination $g$, earliest departure time $t_0$, reservation table $R$, write-to-table flag \textit{commit}
  \Ensure a sequence of arrival time and corridor occupancy intervals; if \textit{commit} is true, those intervals are also written to $R$
  \State place the label $(s,t_0)$ in a priority queue keyed by (time, node id)
         \Comment{ties in time broken by node id, so the order is deterministic}
  \While{the queue is nonempty}
    \State extract the label $(u,t)$ of least key
    \If{$u=g$} \State backtrack to obtain the occupancy intervals; if \textit{commit} is true, write them to $R$ one by one;
         \Return arrival time $t$ \EndIf
    \ForAll{corridors $e=(u,v)$ leaving $u$}
      \State $t_{\text{in}} \gets R.\textsc{EarliestEntry}(e,t,\tau_e)$
         \Comment{wait at the berth of $u$ until $t_{\text{in}}$}
      \State if $t_{\text{in}}+\tau_e$ is strictly earlier than the incumbent arrival at $v$, update the label $(v,\ t_{\text{in}}+\tau_e)$
    \EndFor
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return failure \Comment{should not occur when $G$ is strongly connected; see the text}
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The number of reservations is finite, time is bounded, and $G$ is strongly connected, so a path always exists: in the worst case the vehicle waits until every existing occupancy has ended and then travels along an unimpeded shortest path.
This guarantees that Proposition~\ref{prop:decodable} below holds, so the search need not repair infeasible solutions.
A single call costs $O\bigl(|E|\,(W^{2}+\log|V|)\bigr)$: at most $|E|$ corridors are examined; for each, $O(W)$ candidate entry times are enumerated, each candidate is tested for overlap in $O(W)$ time,
and one heap operation of $O(\log|V|)$ is performed, where $W$ is the mean number of reserved intervals per corridor.
If the reservation table were replaced by an ordered structure supporting a logarithmic-time earliest-entry query, the complexity would drop to $O\bigl(|E|(\log W+\log|V|)\bigr)$; the implementation in this paper uses the linear scan above.

Running the same search on an empty reservation table over all pickup--delivery node pairs yields the unimpeded shortest-path matrix $t^{*}$ of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_III}.
It is used in three places: estimating arrival times by unimpeded time in open-loop configurations, the proximity term in the reassignment neighbourhood, and the transport times of the two-stage baseline; it is also the admissible lower bound used by the pruning of Section~\ref{sec:speedup}.


\subsection{Upper level: encoding and event-driven decoding}
\label{sec:upper}

\subsubsection{Chromosome encoding}
Let the total number of operations be $N$. An individual consists of two segments: a \emph{machine-assignment vector} of length $N$, indexed by a fixed enumeration, whose $k$-th component is the robotic arm chosen for the $k$-th operation from $\Omega_{ji}$;
and an \emph{operation sequence}, a permutation that allows a job to appear more than once---job $j$ appears $n_j$ times, and its $x$-th appearance stands for $O_{jx}$.
The sequence supplies a global order: the relative order of operations that share a robotic arm is read from it.

When $\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}=1$, each job appears once more in the sequence, corresponding to the dummy operation of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_III} (location fixed at $v_0$, processing time zero).
Return transport therefore uses the same assignment and sequencing as intermediate transport, and the decoder needs no separate branch.

Which vehicle a task is given to is not written into the chromosome; it is decided at decode time (Section~\ref{sec:dispatch}).
A third segment for dispatching would enlarge the search space and would no longer guarantee that every chromosome is decodable, so a repair operator would be required.


\subsubsection{Event-driven decoder}
The decoder (Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode}) implements the evaluation path. It sweeps the operation sequence from left to right, maintaining the idle
time of each robotic arm, the current node and ready time of each job, and the current node and available time of each vehicle. When the
arm of an operation differs from the job's current location, a transport task of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tasks} is generated, a vehicle is chosen, and two paths are planned against the \emph{live}
reservation table: an empty leg to the pickup point and a loaded leg to the destination, joined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:load}.
The operation then starts by Eq.~\eqref{eq:bind}; the decoder also records what determined the start---the arrival of the job (waiting for the workpiece) or the completion of the previous operation on that arm (waiting for the arm)---and later tracing of a postponed start takes this record as authoritative.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Decode}: fitness from a complete conflict-free simulation}
\label{alg:decode}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require instance, road network, assignment $x$, operation sequence
\Ensure makespan, timetable, per-corridor yield statistics
\State for every robotic arm set $F_m\gets 0$; for every job set $\mathrm{pos}_j\gets v_0$,
       $\mathrm{ready}_j\gets 0$
\State for every vehicle set $\mathrm{loc}_k\gets v_0$, $\mathrm{avail}_k\gets 0$; clear the reservation table
\ForAll{operations $O_{ji}$ taken in sequence order (dummy operations included)}
  \State $m\gets$ the robotic arm of $O_{ji}$ under $x$; $d\gets \mathrm{loc}(m)$
  \If{$\mathrm{pos}_j=d$}
     \State $A_{ji}\gets \mathrm{ready}_j$ \Comment{successive operations on the same arm; no transport task}
  \Else
     \State choose vehicle $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:dispatch})
     \State $a^{\mathrm{E}}\gets\textsc{Route}(\mathrm{loc}_k,\mathrm{pos}_j,
            \mathrm{avail}_k,R,\text{true})$;\ \ $\ell\gets\max(a^{\mathrm{E}},
            \mathrm{ready}_j)$ \Comment{the empty leg occupies corridors as well}
     \State $A_{ji}\gets\textsc{Route}(\mathrm{pos}_j,d,\ell,R,\text{true})$;\ \
            $\mathrm{loc}_k\gets d$;\ \ $\mathrm{avail}_k\gets A_{ji}$
     \State accumulate yielding time by corridor
  \EndIf
  \State if $F_m>A_{ji}$ then $\texttt{bind}\gets\texttt{machine}$, else
         $\texttt{bind}\gets\texttt{arrive}$ \Comment{record waiting for the arm or waiting for the workpiece}
  \State $S_{ji}\gets\max(A_{ji},F_m)$;\ \ $C_{ji}\gets S_{ji}+p_{ji}$;\ \
         $F_m\gets C_{ji}$;\ \ $\mathrm{pos}_j\gets d$;\ \
         $\mathrm{ready}_j\gets C_{ji}$
\EndFor
\State \Return $C_{\max}=\max_{j,i} C_{ji}$, the timetable, and the per-corridor yield statistics
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Decoding is deterministic: reservations are written in the order in which the decoder generates tasks, and ties in time are broken by a fixed rule at both sites---the routing label queue is keyed by (arrival time, node id) and a label is updated only when the new arrival is strictly earlier;
dispatching scans vehicles in increasing id order and keeps the first strictly better one (Section~\ref{sec:speedup}).
A chromosome therefore always corresponds to a unique timetable, and evaluation is deterministic. The seed-paired, equal-budget comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V} depend on this.

\begin{prop}[Every chromosome is decodable]
  \label{prop:decodable}
  For any machine assignment and any operation sequence, Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} terminates and returns a conflict-free, deadlock-free timetable of finite makespan. The search therefore need not invoke a repair operator.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  By the encoding, the operation sequence is a topological order of each job's precedence constraints: the $x$-th appearance of a job stands for its $x$-th operation, so $\mathrm{ready}_j$ is already known when it is read.
  Ample buffers (A4) mean that an operation is never blocked for lack of storage, so once the job has arrived, Eq.~\eqref{eq:bind} always supplies a finite start time.
  Each \textsc{Route} call returns in finite time by the argument following Algorithm~\ref{alg:route}, and occupies only slots that are still free on the reservation table, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl} holds by construction.
  Deadlock-freedom likewise holds by construction: the corridor is the only contended resource (A6); waits occur only at berths of ample capacity and do not occupy corridors; every occupancy written to the reservation table ends at a finite time, so there is no circular wait among vehicles; in the worst case a vehicle waits until every existing occupancy has ended and then proceeds.
  The sweep executes $N+n\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}$ times, and each time schedules a finite number of events at finite times.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reservation-table-aware dispatching}
\label{sec:dispatch}

A vehicle may be chosen either by an unimpeded-shortest-path estimate or by a trial query on the reservation table. The open-loop rule scores each vehicle by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rule}
\mathrm{score}(k)=\max\bigl(\mathrm{avail}_k+t^{*}(\mathrm{loc}_k,\text{pickup}),\,
\mathrm{ready}_j\bigr)+t^{*}(\text{pickup},\text{destination}),
\end{equation}
which does not consult the reservation table and may therefore select a vehicle that is geometrically near but whose corridors are already occupied.
The formula is the same as the idle-first dispatching of Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework}, and is also a typical single-attribute rule in surveys of multi-AGV dispatching~\cite{lu2022agv}.

The closed-loop default instead uses \emph{reservation-table-aware dispatching}. For each candidate vehicle, the empty leg is planned and temporarily written to the reservation table, the loaded leg is then planned, the earliest feasible delivery time from this trial query is recorded, and the reservation table is rolled back to its state before the trial query.
The empty leg must be written temporarily first; otherwise the loaded leg cannot see that occupancy, the two legs may be placed in the same time slot on the same corridor, and the trial result would be too early.
The vehicle selected is the one with the earliest feasible delivery, not the one that is best by the unimpeded estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule}.

Vehicles have identical capability (assumption~A5) and differ only in current location and corridor occupancy, so contention on the reservation table can directly determine which vehicle is selected.

The cost of the mechanism is that the trial query raises the routing calls per transport task from $2$ to at most $2N_A$, so a single evaluation costs about \CostMultBefore{} times as much as under rule-based dispatching.
Unless this overhead is reduced, the makespan gain of the mechanism is offset by the extra computation under an equal wall-clock budget.
The two speed-ups are given in Section~\ref{sec:speedup}; Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V} therefore compares the two dispatching rules only under the same wall-clock limit.

\subsection{Low overhead: admissible lower-bound pruning and winner-path reuse}
\label{sec:speedup}

Under an equal wall-clock budget, reservation-table-aware dispatching faces the following problem. At an equal number of \emph{generations}, reservation-table-aware dispatching outperforms rule-based dispatching by \GainProbeSameGen{} (Figure~\ref{fig:protocols});
but a single evaluation costs \CostMultBefore{} times as much, so at equal \emph{wall-clock time} this gain is offset by the extra computational overhead and the net effect is close to zero.
Hence, as long as the cost multiple can be reduced without changing any selection, the gain of the mechanism becomes visible. This subsection gives two such speed-ups, both of which \emph{provably do not change the selected vehicle or the timetable}.

\paragraph{Admissible lower-bound pruning.}
Conflict-free routing can only be \emph{later} because of yielding; it can never be faster than the shortest path that ignores contention. The estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule} used by the open-loop rule is therefore an \emph{admissible lower bound} on the realized delivery time:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lb_dispatch}
\mathrm{lb}(k)=\max\bigl(\mathrm{avail}_k+t^{*}(\mathrm{loc}_k,\text{pickup}),\,
\mathrm{ready}_j\bigr)+t^{*}(\text{pickup},\text{destination})\ \le\ \mathrm{est}(k),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{est}(k)$ is the earliest feasible delivery time obtained by a complete two-leg trial query for vehicle $k$.
Hence, if a vehicle's lower bound is already no earlier than the current best trial-query delivery time, routing need not be called for it.

\paragraph{Winner-path reuse.}
After the trial query the reservation table is rolled back as a whole, and the winner's two legs are discarded with it; the decoder then replans the winner on the same (restored) reservation table and writes the legs, causing two redundant routing calls.
Since the search is deterministic, the replanned paths are exactly the same as those found in the trial query, so the paths computed for the winner during the trial query can be cached and written directly to the reservation table without recomputation.

\begin{prop}[The two speed-ups do not change the selected vehicle or the timetable]
  \label{prop:speedup_equiv}
  In the implementation, vehicles are queried in increasing id order, and the selected vehicle is replaced only when some vehicle's feasible delivery is strictly earlier than the current best.
  Under this rule, after the pruning of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lb_dispatch} and winner-path reuse are added, the selected vehicle, the occupancies written to the reservation table, and the final timetable are the same as when a complete two-leg trial query is run for every vehicle.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  Consider pruning first. When vehicle $k$ is reached, let the current best trial-query delivery be $\mathrm{est}^{\ast}$.
  If $\mathrm{lb}(k)\ge \mathrm{est}^{\ast}$, then Eq.~\eqref{eq:lb_dispatch} gives
  $\mathrm{est}(k)\ge\mathrm{lb}(k)\ge\mathrm{est}^{\ast}$,
  so $k$'s feasible delivery cannot be strictly earlier, and $k$ would not be selected even if every vehicle received a complete trial query; not calling routing for it leaves the selected vehicle unchanged.
  The reservation table is saved before each trial query and restored afterwards; vehicles for which routing is not called do not rewrite the table and therefore do not affect later queries.
  Consider reuse next: the two legs of the selected vehicle were computed against one and the same reservation-table state, and the restored table is in the same state that the decoder faces when it subsequently writes.
  Since routing is deterministic, replanning in the same state yields the same paths as the cached ones, so writing the cached paths and replanning before writing produce identical occupancy intervals.
\end{proof}

Together the two speed-ups give a \SpeedupProbe{}-fold speed-up, cut routing calls by \RouteCallCut{}, and reduce the cost multiple of a single evaluation from \CostMultBefore{} to \CostMultAfter{}.
These three numbers are the \emph{joint} effect of the two speed-ups: in the experiments they are measured as one on/off switch (Table~\ref{tab:prune}), and are not attributed separately to pruning or reuse.

The effect of pruning varies with fleet size. The tighter the bound and the earlier a good trial-query delivery time is found, the more vehicles need no routing call; the larger the fleet, the more candidates there are, and the total trial-query cost grows approximately linearly with $N_A$.
Measurements in Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V} show that the pruning effect weakens as the fleet grows, but still cuts \RouteCallCutLo{} of the routing calls on the largest fleet tested.
Taken together, these effects mean that the gain of the mechanism may vanish beyond some fleet size; Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} explains that the present number of seeds is not sufficient to determine that size.

\subsection{Two further inter-layer interfaces: surcharges and yield records}
\label{sec:interface}

What the evaluation path and the dispatching trial query read from the lower level are both \emph{realized durations}, i.e.\ traversal times after conflict-free routing. They rank every candidate by its makespan with its own yielding wait included; methods that schedule first and route once obtain a realized duration only once, after the assignment has been fixed. Realized durations, however, do not indicate which decision caused the wait. We therefore implement two further interfaces that carry richer information and compare them against it under an equal wall-clock budget; the results are in Section~\ref{sec:pricing}.

\paragraph{Return an extra cost on corridor--time slots.}
By analogy with the use of shadow prices to coordinate resources in Lagrangian relaxation and column generation, a surcharge can be attached to the contended resource: each corridor--time slot receives a surcharge $\pi(e,b)$, the amount that a vehicle occupying corridor $e$ in time slot $b$ must count in addition to its arrival time,
so that path planning minimizes ``arrival time $+\theta\cdot$ the sum of surcharges of the slots traversed'' (the surcharge coefficient $\theta\ge0$ is the only tunable parameter), and reassignment must likewise pay the surcharge for the traversal it requires.
We implement two estimates: perturbing the capacity of a single slot and re-solving, and a low-cost approximation refreshed each generation.

Mechanistically, a surcharge provides no new information. The wait caused by contention already enters the vehicle's arrival time, then enters the makespan through Eq.~\eqref{eq:bind}, and is counted by the fitness;
attaching a further cost to the same occupancy counts the same delay twice. Later tasks not yet planned may be blocked by reservations written earlier, but that effect is likewise already reflected in the makespan after a complete decode.
Hence, as long as the fitness is taken from a complete conflict-free decode, attaching a congestion cost to the lower-level objective adds no information. We verify this inference only on this one interface.

The main overhead of a surcharge follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:label}: once the surcharge is a second criterion, earlier arrival is no longer always better, and single-label Dijkstra must be replaced by a multi-label Pareto search,
so the cost of a single evaluation rises whether or not the surcharge is non-zero.


\paragraph{Return the yield record.}
The other choice is to return structure. When a yield occurs, path planning already knows the corridor, the time slot, and the transport that first occupied that corridor; the decoder keeps these yield events, not only the total wait.
A record is a triple (corridor, time slot, preceding occupying transport), used to decide which operations local search should change.
It does not enter any objective: the lower level still minimizes arrival time, the upper level still minimizes makespan; only the way the neighbourhood is generated changes.
There is therefore no double-counting of delay. The operators are given in Section~\ref{sec:ls}.

\subsection{Yield-record-guided local search}
\label{sec:ls}
This subsection writes the yield record of the previous subsection as local-search operators, applied to the top-ranked individuals in each generation; all four configurations of the main batch enable it with the same setting. Its critical-chain tracing is also used for the reading of the case study in Section~\ref{sec:casestudy}.

\paragraph{Critical-chain tracing.}
Backtracking from the event that determines the makespan yields a chain. A chain that contains only operations cannot explain why a start was postponed: when the start is waiting for the workpiece, the postponement may come from an unfinished upstream operation, no vehicle being available, or corridor contention, and the three call for different changes.
The backtrack therefore includes transport legs as well, and labels every link with a type (Table~\ref{tab:attrib}): a yield on a loaded leg is recorded as corridor occupancy; an empty leg is classified as ``vehicle'' or ``corridor'' by the share of yielding time in that leg's duration, with threshold $0.5$.
Only the corridor-occupancy class gives a concrete corridor, time slot, and preceding occupying transport, and this is exactly the information that local search uses.

\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{2.55cm}@{}}
\toprule
Label & Criterion & Remedy \\

\paragraph{Two families of operators and the acceptance rule.}
\emph{Reassignment: change the processing location.} For at most $L=5$ real operations $O_{ji}$ on the critical chain (with current assignment on robotic arm $m$), each alternative robotic arm
$m'\in\Omega_{ji}\setminus\{m\}$ is scored by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reassign}
\mathrm{score}(m')=t^{*}\bigl(d_{j,i-1},\mathrm{loc}(m')\bigr)
+t^{P}_{jim'},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the sum of the unimpeded travel time from the node $d_{j,i-1}$ of the previous operation to $m'$ and the processing time on $m'$; the lowest-scoring arm produces a neighbour that changes only that one assignment. This allows a longer processing time to be traded for a shorter path with better traffic conditions, which is the reassignment trade-off of this paper.
The score contains no cumulative-yield term, because that term is a total over the whole fleet and the whole planning horizon and is not commensurable with the scale of a single operation.

\emph{Staggering: change the order.} Take the longest corridor-occupancy link on the chain and generate two directed neighbours: advance the blocked operation in the sequence, or postpone the preceding occupying operation, so that the two occupancies no longer overlap.
A move only swaps adjacent appearances of different jobs, leaving the internal order of each job unchanged, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:decodable} the result remains decodable.

The neighbourhood therefore has at most $L+2=7$ candidates, each specified by the yield record. The first improvement is accepted, for at most three rounds; each round \emph{recomputes} the critical chain from the current solution, because after a successful change the chain that determines the makespan often changes with it (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ls}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{ContentionGuidedSearch}: yield-record-guided local search}
\label{alg:ls}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require chromosome $c$ and its decode result $r$
\Ensure a chromosome whose makespan is no worse than $r.C_{\max}$, and its decode result
\For{$\textit{round}=1$ \textbf{to} $3$}
  \State $\mathcal{N}\gets\textsc{Reassign}(c,r)\ \cup\ \textsc{Stagger}(c,r)$
     \Comment{at most $L+2$ neighbours, each specified by the yield record}
  \State \textit{improved} $\gets$ \textbf{false}
  \ForAll{$c'\in\mathcal{N}$}
    \State $r'\gets\textsc{Decode}(c')$
    \If{$r'.C_{\max}<r.C_{\max}$}
      \State $c\gets c'$;\ \ $r\gets r'$;\ \ \textit{improved} $\gets$
             \textbf{true};\ \ \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{\textbf{not} \textit{improved}} \State \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $c,r$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Outer genetic algorithm}

The outer layer uses a conventional population search.
The initial population is generated at random: a robotic arm is drawn uniformly from $\Omega_{ji}$, and the sequence is shuffled; two further individuals are added, one assigning every operation to its fastest arm and one assigning greedily for load balance.
Selection is by tournament with a small elite. For crossover, the sequence segment uses order crossover that preserves precedence, and the assignment segment uses uniform crossover; mutation either swaps two positions in the sequence or moves an operation to another optional robotic arm.
Fitness is the decoded makespan. Hyperparameters are given in Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

\subsection{Complexity and cost}

One fitness evaluation processes $N+n\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}$ events. Under closed-loop dispatching each transport uses at most $2N_A$ routing calls, so each individual costs
$O\bigl((N+n)\,N_A\,|E|\,(W^{2}+\log|V|)\bigr)$; open-loop selection by unimpeded time no longer carries the factor $N_A$.
The two speed-ups of Section~\ref{sec:speedup} change neither this worst-case bound nor the selected vehicle or the timetable (Proposition~\ref{prop:speedup_equiv}); measured, they cut routing calls by \RouteCallCut{} in total (Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V}; between \RouteCallCutLo{} and \RouteCallCutHi{} across instances), which amounts to replacing $N_A$ by a smaller effective multiple.
Local search runs at most three rounds with at most $L+2$ neighbours per round, so it adds at most $3\times(L+2)=21$ further decodes for each selected individual.

Within the framework, the cost of a single evaluation can differ by about two orders of magnitude across configurations. On the ladder batch the measurements are: open-loop \MsOpen{}\,ms, closed-loop rule-based dispatching \MsLoopRule{}\,ms, and full closed-loop \MsLoopProbe{}\,ms, the closed loop being about $37$ times the open loop.
The surcharge is not in that batch; it comes from an earlier surcharge scan, within which turning the surcharge on raises a single evaluation from \MsPricedRef{}\,ms to \MsPriced{}\,ms, a further factor of about $4.8$.
The absolute timings of the two batches are not directly comparable, but the product of the two multiples shows that the configurations with the highest and lowest single-evaluation cost differ by about two orders of magnitude.
Equal generation counts are therefore not an appropriate comparison, and Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V} aligns wall-clock time instead.

Under a fixed budget, a more expensive single evaluation means fewer evaluations can be completed. Whether an interface converts into a makespan gain cannot be deduced from its design in advance; it must be measured under an equal wall-clock budget (Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_V}).

The results above guarantee correctness, not solution quality: Proposition~\ref{prop:decodable}, Lemma~\ref{lem:label}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:speedup_equiv} guarantee that every candidate is feasible, that its evaluation is consistent with the conflict model of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_III}, and that the two speed-ups do not change any selection; together with the deterministic decoding of Section~\ref{sec:upper}, the solution procedure is reproducible.
As with most metaheuristics, solution quality has no theoretical bound and is evaluated experimentally in the next section.
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:Chapter_V}

This section answers four questions. How large is the gain from including yielding wait in the fitness (the closed loop)? How much further does reservation-table-aware dispatching reduce makespan, can it replace the closed loop, and do the two amplify each other when used together?
Over what range of instances do these gains hold, and \emph{can the present number of seeds determine} that range? Why do the other interfaces that we implemented fail to yield a net gain?

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:instances}

\subsubsection{Controlled instance families}
FJSP\_PT benchmarks usually write travel times as a matrix indexed by location pairs; it records only the total duration between endpoints and supplies no corridor network.
Of the \PubNLayout{} layouts used by Homayouni and Fontes~\cite{homayouni2021production},
\PubNLayoutAsym{} matrices are entirely asymmetric (consistent with one-way tracks), and \PubNLayoutBad{} of them also violate the triangle inequality.
Asymmetry rules out undirected corridors of the kind used here (one $\tau_e$ per corridor, so the resulting $t^{*}$ must be symmetric);
violating the triangle inequality is stronger still: the all-pairs shortest paths of any graph satisfy the triangle inequality, so these \PubNLayoutBad{} matrices cannot be recovered even if directed graphs are allowed.
A network reverse-engineered from such a matrix would be a different instance, and the published reference values would no longer be comparable.
We therefore generate a set of controlled instances: the road network is given explicitly, and each feature of Section~\ref{sec:features} can be varied on its own.
A separate comparison on public instances---with the contention constraint removed, so that the problem reverts to the same mathematical problem as in the literature---is given in Section~\ref{sec:external}; it calibrates the strength of the upper-level search, not our framework.

All instances share the same core: $12$ jobs, $3$ operations per job for $36$ real operations, $8$ robotic arms,
heterogeneity $H=0.3$, and a transport-to-processing ratio $\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p$ scaled to $4.0$, so that vehicle contention is the main constraint.
$H$ is $0.3$ on all ten instances and does not vary with flexibility.
For each operation, the generator first draws independent perturbations of its $|\Omega_{ji}|$ processing times, standardizes them to zero mean and unit variance, and then multiplies them by $H$; the within-row coefficient of variation is therefore independent of $|\Omega_{ji}|$, and changing $F$ in Family~C does not change $H$.
Small deviations caused by rounding are measured after generation and recorded with each instance.
On this core, the instances vary along three factors (Table~\ref{tab:instances}).
Every instance is generated with a fixed random seed and is fully reproducible.

\emph{Family A: layout (contention structure).}
The first three instances use a spine layout: a main corridor (the spine) runs through the arm area, and each arm hangs off it on its own branch corridor at one of two hubs on the spine, half near the load/unload point $v_0$ and half at the far end of the spine; $v_0$ sits at one end of the spine and connects to it through one or two parallel exits.
This matches the spine layout common in high-volume plants---processing areas line both sides of a central corridor, in-plant moves travel along it, and congestion on that corridor is a known shop-floor problem~\cite{suh2022spine}.
Real shops usually place the store or load/unload point where the trips to all stations are roughly balanced, to shorten transport and spread corridor occupancy; we place $v_0$ at one end of the spine, so every transport must pass the same exit and the same stretch of the main corridor. This is a deliberately high-contention case, and it is exactly the case the closed-loop mechanism is meant to handle.
We also choose the spine layout because it concentrates contention on parallel lanes that can be added or removed: adding a lane changes capacity only, not distance. A tandem single-vehicle loop keeps vehicles from meeting each other~\cite{rahimikelarijani2020tandem}; it has almost no contention and does not suit the purpose of this study.

The spine layout covers only the high-contention range. Covering the low-contention range needs another topology, the \emph{grid}: with more intersections and more detours, an occupied corridor can be bypassed and contention falls, but the trip from the load/unload point to the farthest arm becomes longer.
The five instances therefore split into two topologies: \texttt{funnel}, \texttt{high}, and \texttt{mid} use the spine layout; \texttt{low} and \texttt{scatter} are grids.
Contention intensity runs from \ContentionAHi{} to \ContentionLo{}, but is not monotone in this construction order (\texttt{low} is above \texttt{mid}; per-instance values are in Table~\ref{tab:instances}).
The paper-wide maximum contention, \ContentionHi{}, is not in this family. It belongs to the instance that raises flexibility to $F=1.0$ (every operation may use every arm) on the same \texttt{funnel} layout, which is part of Family~C below.

The three spine instances differ only in their parallel lanes ($1\to2$ exits at the load/unload point, $1\to2$ lanes in the middle segment). Each lane is a two-hop path of equal total duration, so adding or removing one changes only how many vehicles can pass in parallel, not the distance; the node/corridor counts rise from $13/12$ to $15/16$.
\texttt{funnel} and \texttt{high} differ only by one parallel exit at the load/unload point, which halves the minimum cut; they form a single-factor contrast.
The two grid instances switch to a $4\times4$ grid ($16/24$), and to a scattered layout on the same grid that places the arms at the farthest points. The two have the same size and edge weights and differ only in arm placement; $v_0$ again sits at a corner.

The unimpeded trip from the load/unload point to the farthest arm is \LegDumbbell{} on all three spine instances and \LegGrid{} on both grid instances, nearly twice as long.
The two grid instances also have the highest makespans in the family, even though their contention intensity is the lowest.
This family is therefore a splice of ``capacity $+$ topology'', not an end-to-end single-factor scan: differences among the three spine instances can be attributed to capacity, while crossing over to the grid changes capacity and distance together.
When Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} orders the instances by contention intensity, two inversions appear: one stems from this topological splice, and the other lies inside the spine layout and needs a separate explanation.
Contention intensity is the quantity with which we directly describe congestion, and this family spans the widest range of it, so that section's judgement on where the closed loop applies rests mainly on this family.

\emph{Family B: vehicle-to-arm ratio $N_A/N_M$.}
On \texttt{funnel}, take $N_A\in\{4,8,12,16\}$, i.e.\ ratios of $0.5$, $1.0$, $1.5$, and $2.0$; $1.5$ is the base point shared by the three families (listed as \texttt{A funnel} under Family~A in the table).
This family tests whether dispatching has an upper limit in fleet size (Sections~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} and~\ref{sec:costlaw}).

\emph{Family C: flexibility $F$.}
On the same layout, take $F\in\{0.3,\,0.6,\,1.0\}$, i.e.\ the fraction of arms each operation may use; $0.6$ is again the base point.
$|\Omega_{ji}|$ is randomized and rounded so that its expectation equals $F\cdot N_M$; the measured base point is $0.61$ (Table~\ref{tab:instances} lists measured values; the other two levels match their targets).
Flexibility sets the scope of reassignment, and also how far corridor contention can be turned into an adjustment of the assignment.

The three families comprise $\NCases$ instances. The base point \texttt{A funnel} is at once the $1.5$ of Family~B and the $0.6$ of Family~C; it is generated and run only once, and occupies a single row in Tables~\ref{tab:instances} and~\ref{tab:bytag}.
Hence $5+3+2=\NCases$, while the Family~B and Family~C scans have four and three levels, respectively. Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} must count the base point when it reads these two families; leaving it out would make them look more monotone than they are.

Contention intensity is determined by the instance structure. It is measured on the initial random population ($20$ individuals) as the mean share of yielding wait in travel time; per-instance values are in Table~\ref{tab:instances}.
We do not use optimized solutions, because they reflect the solution rather than the instance: the search routes around congested corridors, contention tends to zero after convergence, and the differences among instances disappear.
Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} uses this quantity to order the instances.

\paragraph{Comparison with public instances.}
The $H$, $F$, and $\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p$ of our generated instances need an external reference.
The features of the \PubNInst{} public instances in Section~\ref{sec:external} are computed with the same feature function and the same convention, so the two can be compared directly.
Median flexibility is \PubFMed{}; our base point of $0.6$ is about one and a half times that, and the $F=0.3$ of Family~C falls inside the public range;
median heterogeneity is only \PubHMed{}, the largest of the \PubNInst{} instances is \PubHMax{}, and none reaches our $H=0.3$;
the public maximum of $\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p$ is only \PubTtTpHi{}, while we scale it to $4.0$.

The last two are both extrapolations, but with different meanings.
$\bar{T}_t/\bar{T}_p=4.0$ is deliberate: if transport is only a few percent of processing time, vehicle contention need not be modelled separately, and the common FJSP\_PT instances are of this kind.
$H=0.3$ lies above the literature range; if $H$ raised the closed-loop gain, part of the differences among configurations would come from this value.
Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} tests this with independent data spanning four levels of $H$, and the gain does not rise with $H$; the instances and baselines of that batch differ from the main batch, so it supports only this directional conclusion.

\subsubsection{Comparison protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The comparison rests on the following rules.

\paragraph{Common executor: report the realized makespan.}
The makespan that the open loop schedules from unimpeded travel times is often infeasible on the real corridors.
Every configuration's schedule is therefore executed by the same conflict-free router and then checked by a validator independent of the decoder; what is compared is always the realized makespan after execution.
The gap between the open loop's self-reported and realized values (hereafter, the optimism margin) is especially large on the funnel layout.
If each configuration were compared on its own self-reported value, the open loop would gain an advantage from a makespan that cannot be realized.

\paragraph{Equal wall-clock budget, not equal generation count.}
A single evaluation does different work in each configuration: the open loop looks up the constant matrix $t^{*}$; the closed loop runs conflict-free routing for every candidate; the surcharge interface additionally needs a multi-label search.
The cost of a single evaluation can differ by about two orders of magnitude. Comparing at an equal generation count would give the more expensive configuration more compute, and an apparent mechanism gain might be only a larger budget.
We therefore align wall-clock time: all configurations on the same instance share the same budget, $90$ seconds in the main batch, with a cap of $2000$ generations and early stopping after $400$ consecutive generations without improvement.
The generation cap never fires. Every open-loop run is ended by early stopping after a little over four hundred generations, using on average about one fifth of the budget; both closed-loop configurations use the full $90$ seconds every time.
This setting favours the baseline: the open loop is not cut off by the budget but searches until it stops improving, and in its shorter wall-clock time it completes as many evaluations as closed-loop rule-based dispatching, or more (Section~\ref{sec:costlaw}).
Each remaining batch answers one specific question; their instances, seeds, and budgets are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:batches}.

Aligning wall-clock time lets the configuration with the cheaper evaluation complete more evaluations.
Every configuration therefore reports both its cost per evaluation and the number of evaluations actually completed, and Section~\ref{sec:costlaw} analyses the trade-off between the two.

\paragraph{Seeds and paired tests.}
A single seed cannot support a conclusion: on the same instance and configuration, three seeds produced a range of $11$ makespan units, larger than any mechanism effect being measured.
Each instance is therefore run with $\NSeeds$ seeds, giving $\NCases\times\NSeeds=\NPairs$ pairs in total, and a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test is performed with pairing by seed.
$\NSeeds$ seeds are enough to resolve an effect of a few percentage points when the $\NPairs$ pairs are pooled, but not inside a single instance.
Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} shows that this boundary falls exactly between the closed loop and dispatching; per-instance conclusions are confined to this power.

\paragraph{Multiple comparisons and effect sizes.}
The paper reports a little over twenty Wilcoxon tests. Without correction, the chance of ``at least one significant instance out of ten'' is about $40\%$ under the global null, not the nominal $5\%$.
Every family of tests is corrected within that family, and the family is stated where the result is reported.
There are two kinds of family: the five pooled contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:main} form one family; in Table~\ref{tab:bytag}, the $\NCases$ instances of each effect form one family.
We use the Holm correction to control the chance of at least one Type~I error within a family; Benjamini--Hochberg is computed as well, and any case where the two disagree is marked where it occurs.

There are two exceptions. First, when Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} reports the vehicle-to-arm ratio, it uses uncorrected $p$-values and marks each one: these values are already non-significant before correction, and correction would only make the conclusion more conservative.
Second, the independent data on \MatrixCells{} instances cited in Sections~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} and~\ref{sec:pricing} take their count of significant instances from that batch's own report, without correction under our rules; they serve only to confirm direction and magnitude, and every mention of that count should be read as an uncorrected count.
Apart from these two cases, every $p$-value in this paper is corrected.

Correction only answers whether the direction can be distinguished from zero. Each pooled contrast also reports a Hodges--Lehmann pseudomedian with a $95\%$ distribution-free confidence interval. These come from the same source as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test: the point estimate, the interval, and the $p$-value are all read from the same null distribution.
The mean pairwise relative gain, the main figure in our tables, is convenient for adding across tables, but it does not come from the rank test and carries no precision.
We give both. For example, the mean for dispatching inside the closed loop is \GainProbeLoop{} and the pseudomedian is \HLProbeLoop{}; the gap comes from pairs on the two vehicle-to-arm-ratio instances where makespan worsens (positive gain), and the pseudomedian is insensitive to such values.
Error bars in the figures are the standard error of the mean pairwise relative gain, shaded bands are the seed-to-seed range, and tables do not report $\pm$.

\paragraph{Correctness.}
For each solution, a validator independent of the decoder reconstructs the corridor occupancies from the timetable and compares the result with the composite lower bound.
Runs that fail the check or fall below the bound are reported separately and are not included in any instance mean.

\subsubsection{Implementation and environment}
\label{sec:impl}
The experiments run single-threaded on an Intel Xeon W-1270 @ $3.40$\,GHz. The implementation is pure Python ($\ge 3.9.6$) using only the standard library, with no third-party dependencies and no solver.
The hyperparameters of the outer genetic algorithm and the local search take their defaults, are not tuned, and are identical across configurations: population $60$, crossover probability $0.8$, mutation probability $0.2$, elite $5$, binary tournament; the local search acts each generation on the top $10\%$ of individuals, examines at most $L=5$ critical operations per round, and runs at most three rounds.
Because the hyperparameters are conventional and identical across configurations, the measured gains come from the mechanisms rather than from operator tuning.
Absolute runtimes cannot be compared with those of a compiled implementation. A faster implementation would let every configuration complete more evaluations within the same budget, but would not change the ratio of cost per evaluation between configurations; this ratio is measured between configurations that share the same implementation.

\subsection{Four-level baseline ladder}
\label{sec:arms}

The comparison must answer two questions separately: how much the makespan differs when the structure is aligned with the pipeline structures common in the literature, and how much it differs when only one component is changed.
The former requires structural correspondence; the latter requires changing one thing at a time.
Ablations alone cannot be mapped onto the common pipeline structures, while a literature comparison alone would credit the gain of conflict-free routing itself to our mechanisms.
We therefore use a four-level ladder in which each level corresponds to a clearly identifiable structure.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,topsep=3pt,itemsep=3pt]
\item[B0: open-loop planning + realized execution.]
The upper level searches by unimpeded shortest-path times (the search ignores conflicts); after the search, the plan is executed by the same conflict-free router.
This represents the open-loop structure: the scheduling layer produces a plan that ignores transport contention, and a downstream conflict-free router executes it. FJSP\_PT methods that schedule on a constant matrix~\cite{meng2025cp,qin2026knowledge,fu2025integrated} fall into this class when executed on a real road network, as does the framework of Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework}. Dispatching is idle-first, the same as Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule} used by rule-based dispatching in our framework.
\item[B0+: open-loop planning + reservation-table-aware dispatching.]
The schedule is likewise fixed by unimpeded travel times; only vehicle selection is changed to a trial query on the reservation table.
This represents the two-stage structure: the first stage schedules on a \emph{constant travel-time matrix}, and only the second stage optimizes vehicle assignment and handles conflicts on the fixed schedule, with no feedback (e.g.\ Sun et al.~\cite{sun2023integrated}).
Here, reservation-table-aware dispatching serves as a greedy approximation of that second stage.
\item[B1: closed-loop planning + rule-based dispatching.]
Fitness during the search is taken from conflict-free routing, while dispatching still estimates times from unimpeded shortest paths via Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule}. It differs from B2 only in the vehicle-selection rule.
\item[B2: closed-loop planning + reservation-table-aware dispatching.]
Our method.
\end{description}

The four levels are in fact a $2\times2$ design along two factors, not a linear sequence. The first factor is whether the search includes conflict-induced delay (open-loop / closed-loop); the second is whether a vehicle is selected by unimpeded time or by a trial query on the reservation table (rule / trial query).
If two configurations differ in both factors at once, their difference cannot be decomposed.
For example, $\text{B0+}\to\text{B1}$ crosses both factors and \textbf{cannot} be used to measure the closed loop.
The interpretable contrasts fix one factor and change only the other, i.e.\ the four pairs that share a row or a column of Table~\ref{tab:arms}.

How B0 is executed decides how strong the baseline is. The plan found by the open-loop search already contains a set of vehicle assignments, and it can be reproduced at execution time in two ways:
\emph{replay the plan} executes with the vehicles the original plan specifies; \emph{on-site reassignment} re-selects vehicles by the rule at execution time.
The former is measured to be stronger ($63.00$ versus $78.00$ on the funnel instance) and matches the practice of Li et al.~\cite{li2026framework}, whose task-allocation layer fixes the robots on the Gantt chart first and, at execution, only shifts the time axis without reassignment.
The main report uses the stronger replay-the-plan convention; on-site reassignment is listed alongside.

\paragraph{Reservation-table-aware dispatching.}
Fix the open or closed loop and change only the vehicle-selection rule. \emph{Inside the closed loop},
$\text{B1}\to\text{B2}$ is $-\GainProbeLoop$ ($p\PProbeLoop$), with pseudomedian \HLProbeLoop{} and

The two levels share the same scheduling search and the same budget; they differ only in whether vehicle selection uses a trial query on the reservation table.
This is a pooled result. The interval does not cross zero, but its lower end is less than one percentage point from zero; after family-wise correction no instance is significant (\CellsSigProbe{} before correction), and two of the ten instances have a positive sign, i.e.\ B2 is slower than B1 at vehicle-to-arm ratios $1.0$ and $2.0$ (Table~\ref{tab:bytag}).

Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion} discusses this resolution limit.

\emph{Under the open loop}, the same rule gives $-\GainProbeOpen$ ($p\PProbeOpen$), likewise significant.
It measures the step from the open-loop structure to the two-stage structure;
its magnitude (\GainProbeOpen{}) is far smaller than that of the closed loop (\GainLoopRule{}).
Reservation-table-aware dispatching is significant under both the open and the closed loop, but its effect is much smaller and cannot by itself explain the $\text{B0}\to\text{B2}$ reduction.

\paragraph{End to end, and how the two effects combine.}
\label{sec:complement}
$\text{B0}\to\text{B2}$ is $-\GainEndToEnd$ when pooled
($p\PEndToEnd$; of the $\NPairs$ pairs, $\WinEndToEnd$ wins, $\LoseEndToEnd$ losses, and $1$ tie).
That is, moving from open-loop planning with rule-based dispatching to closed-loop planning with reservation-table-aware dispatching lowers makespan by about one fifth. The three effects have different reference points, so they cannot simply be added.
If the closed loop and dispatching were independent, the end-to-end gain would equal the compound of the closed loop under rule-based dispatching ($\GainLoopRule$) and dispatching under the open loop ($\GainProbeOpen$), i.e.\ $\GainIndepProduct$. The measured $\GainEndToEnd$ is slightly below this, which shows that each mechanism contributes on its own and \emph{the two do not amplify each other}: B2 is best end to end because the two gains add up.

\paragraph{Relation to ``schedule first, then resolve conflicts''.}
Dispatching still yields a significant $\GainProbeOpen$ under the open loop, so the shortfall of this approach lies not in second-stage dispatching but in the first stage ignoring yielding. Placed in a two-stage structure, the same dispatching rule gains only as much as open-loop dispatching, far less than the closed loop's $\GainLoopRule$; to obtain the closed-loop reduction, yielding wait must enter the fitness during the search. These gaps were measured on our instance families.

\subsection{Three-factor scans and per-instance resolution}
\label{sec:whichcongestion}

The aggregate comparison of Section~\ref{sec:main} rests on $\NCases$ instances. This section takes them apart by layout, vehicle-to-arm ratio, and flexibility.
The trend of the closed loop can be read by family; dispatching cannot be distinguished from zero on any single instance. This section reports that difference. Each of the three families corresponds to an expectation that can be stated in advance.

\emph{Layout (Family A)} tests whether the gain grows with contention intensity: both paths target the delay caused by vehicles yielding to one another, and when contention is weak this delay is small, so their effect is limited.

\emph{Vehicle-to-arm ratio (Family B)} tests whether there is an upper bound on fleet size beyond which overhead offsets the gain. Trial-query cost grows approximately linearly with the fleet, and the pruning of Section~\ref{sec:speedup} has its lowest cut rate on the instance with the largest fleet.

\emph{Flexibility (Family C)} tests whether the gain depends on room for reassignment.

\paragraph{Per-instance resolution.}
The significance marks of Table~\ref{tab:bytag} decide which instances may be read by sign.

\emph{Layout family.}

\paragraph{An independent batch of self-generated instances.}
The results above come from one instance family and one baseline. As a cross-check we use another batch of self-generated instances with contention (public benchmarks are treated in Section~\ref{sec:external}, where contention has been removed): $\MatrixCells$ instances $\times$ $10$ seeds, $\MatrixRuns$ runs in all.
Both the instance core and the baseline differ: the baseline is two-stage surrogate transport, and the batch crosses four contention levels with four heterogeneity levels.
The \emph{integration gain} (two-stage $\to$ closed-loop) is \GainIntegMatrix{}, significant on \SigIntegMatrix{} of the $\MatrixCells$ instances and ranging from \GainIntegMatrixLo{} to \GainIntegMatrixHi{}, consistent in direction and magnitude with the closed loop in this section.
Counts of significant instances in this batch are uncorrected (see Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\paragraph{Heterogeneity does not raise the closed-loop gain.}
The four $H$ levels of that batch answer the question left open in Section~\ref{sec:instances}. Grouping the $\MatrixCells$ instances into four levels by $H$ (four instances per level, one at each contention level), the integration gain is \GainIntegHZero{} at $H=0$, \GainIntegHLow{} at $H=0.15$ (measured \MatrixHMeasLow{}), \GainIntegHMid{} at $H=0.3$, and \GainIntegHHigh{} at $H=0.5$; the rank correlation between gain and $H$ is a weakly negative \RhoIntegH{}.

The measured $H=\MatrixHMeasLow$ lies near the public-instance median \PubHMed{}, and its gain \GainIntegHLow{} differs from the \GainIntegHMid{} at $H=0.3$ by less than half a percentage point: setting $H$ above the literature range did not raise the closed-loop gain.
The expectation that the gain rises with $H$ does not hold: the trend is not monotone, and the rank correlation is negative. Each level has only $4$ instances, and the within-level spread (\GainIntegMatrixLo{} to \GainIntegMatrixHi{}) is far larger than the gaps between levels. The data therefore support only ``the gain does not rise with $H$'' and are not sufficient to assert that $H=0.5$ is lower.
This is compatible with the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:features}: as $H$ grows, processing-time differences among eligible arms gradually outweigh differences in corridor accessibility, so assignment is decided more and more by processing speed and less and less by traffic conditions.
$H=0$ removes the trade-off, not the opportunity: all arms in $\Omega_{ji}$ have the same processing time, so reassignment need not trade processing time for travel time and becomes a pure congestion decision at zero cost. This level has the highest gain of the four (\GainIntegHZero{}), consistent with the definition.
Thus $H$ turns arm selection into a trade-off (without heterogeneity, the reversal of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_I} would not occur), but it does not amplify the closed-loop gain. The closed-loop gain comes from whether the search accounts for yielding; contention strength is set by layout and fleet, not by $H$.

For reservation-table-aware dispatching, that batch gives \GainProbePre{} ($0$ instances significant), the opposite direction from the main batch. The batch ran before the two speed-ups; see Section~\ref{sec:costlaw} for the reason.

\subsection{Ablations and controlled negative controls}
\label{sec:pricing}

This section reports measurements of the two interfaces of Section~\ref{sec:interface}. The surcharge is off by default ($\theta=0$). Yield-record-guided local search is enabled with the same setting in all four configurations of the main batch. It makes no stable contribution to makespan (see the ablation below), but because it is identical across configurations, it does not affect the comparisons between them.

\subsubsection{Yield-record-guided local search}
On the $\MatrixCells$-instance batch of Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion}, turning off yield-record feedback gives a relative gain of \GainCertMatrix{} (significant on only $1$ of the $\MatrixCells$ instances), and turning off the staggering operator gives \GainStaggerMatrix{} (significant on $2$ instances). Neither is positive: under an equal wall-clock budget, decodes spent on this neighbourhood are better spent on population evolution. The mechanism is explained in Section~\ref{sec:whycost}.

These two ablations may cite that earlier batch, but the dispatching ablation may not. The yield record and staggering were not changed before or after the two speed-ups, and their cost does not vary with pruning; the cost of dispatching is exactly what the speed-ups target.

\subsubsection{Surcharge on corridor--time slots}

That argument shows that the surcharge adds no information, but it cannot give the size of the makespan loss, which must therefore be measured separately.
We scan $\theta$ under both budget conventions and fix the search width of path planning at every $\theta$, so that the effect of the surcharge is not confounded with ``how many entry times are examined''.

The measurements are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:theta}. Under equal wall-clock time, every $\theta$ tried makes the makespan worse, and significantly so; the worsening is flat over a ten-fold range of $\theta$. Under an equal number of evaluations, the same comparison cannot be distinguished from zero.
Routing with a surcharge completes about one fifth as many evaluations within the same budget as routing without it: the surcharge does not mis-rank candidate solutions, but it sharply reduces the number of evaluations within the same budget.

Two further observations rule out other explanations. First, $\theta$ is not an effective tuning parameter on this problem. Over two orders of magnitude, routing with a surcharge modifies the same few paths, and both the cumulative surcharge and the returned makespan stay the same. The cumulative surcharge is only a small fraction of total travel time, not enough to change the order of arrival times in $\text{arrival time}+\theta\cdot\text{surcharge}$. Only when $\theta$ is so large that the surcharge outweighs the arrival time does the double counting take effect, and then makespan worsens sharply.
Second, the overhead is structural and is not caused by a non-zero surcharge. The runtime ratio of the same decode with and without a surcharge is the same at every $\theta$; the extra cost comes from ``maintaining a Pareto front at every node and trying several entry times on every arc'', regardless of whether a surcharge is actually applied. This agrees with Lemma~\ref{lem:label}: once a second criterion is introduced there is no longer a total-order dominance, and single-label search must be replaced by multi-label search.

The two budget conventions disagree on the size of the loss but agree on its sign. Whether the surcharge comes from a finite difference of corridor capacity or from a critical-chain proxy, the delay it targets is already counted in the arrival time of the yielding vehicle. As long as fitness is taken from a complete conflict-free decode, the surcharge carries no new information, and the comparison is decided by the overhead of the multi-label search it requires.
Hence, before extending the inter-layer interface, one should first check whether the objective of the receiving layer already contains the quantity the interface would pass; if it does, such an extension is harmful under our protocol.

\subsubsection{Dilution and overhead}
\label{sec:whycost}

Why the yield record and staggering fail to offset their overhead can be established by measurement; the same measurement also explains why the overhead of reservation-table-aware dispatching had to be reduced first.

\paragraph{Allocation of decodes.}
Yield-record-guided local search consumes a large share of all decodes, yet fewer than one in fifty of the neighbours it decodes improve makespan. These improvements are real, but the same decodes spent on population evolution yield more.

The neighbourhood is small and well targeted by construction (Section~\ref{sec:ls}). A low improvement rate despite good targeting means that the operations named by the record do not shorten the critical chain after reassignment or staggering.
We tried three refinements: scoring reassignment by the expected yielding wait along the path rather than by unimpeded shortest-path time; replacing the adjacent swap of staggering with a directed insertion; and accepting plateau moves that ``leave makespan unchanged but lower total yielding wait''. Each was compared independently with the original operator under an equal number of evaluations, and \emph{none} was significant. The failure therefore lies in the signal itself, not in how the signal is used.

\paragraph{Dilution along the critical chain.}
A yield record names the corridor and the time slot of a yield and the transport that occupied that corridor first, that is, the information needed to remove \emph{that} event.
Makespan is determined by a chain. Once one corridor delay is removed, another constraint (an upstream operation, a robotic arm, or another corridor) usually becomes tight, so the realized improvement is only part of the delay removed.
This is why the acceptance rule re-extracts the critical chain after every acceptance. When a bottleneck is only one of several of comparable magnitude, feedback that names it improves makespan by far less than the yielding time it removes.

\paragraph{Why dispatching gives different results before and after the speed-ups.}
The same dilution acts on reservation-table-aware dispatching. The trial query selects the vehicle that delivers the current task earliest, which is a local improvement; only part of it survives when it propagates through the whole critical chain to makespan.
After dilution the reduction is still positive, but only a few percentage points. Before the speed-ups, a single evaluation cost \CostMultBefore{} times as much as under rule-based dispatching, too few evaluations fit into the same wall-clock time, and the net effect could not be distinguished from noise (the independent batch of Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion}).
Only after Section~\ref{sec:speedup} lowered the cost to \CostMultAfter{} times did this reduction become significant in the pooled data.

Under a fixed budget, two quantities that can be measured and improved separately must be considered together: the makespan reduction that remains after dilution, and the extra cost per evaluation.
The two mechanisms fail for different reasons. Yield-record-guided local search is limited by the former: too little reduction survives dilution, and none of the three neighbourhood refinements reaches significance under an equal number of evaluations.
Dispatching is limited by the latter: the post-dilution reduction is always present, and it was initially not significant because the cost per evaluation was too high, a cost that can be lowered without changing the selected vehicle or the timetable.
This paper therefore lists dispatching as a mechanism contribution and reports yield-record-guided local search only as a controlled negative control.


\subsection{The cost law and the effect of the two speed-ups}
\label{sec:costlaw}

Under a fixed wall-clock budget, the cost per evaluation and the number of evaluations trade off against each other. The same dispatching rule has a net effect indistinguishable from noise before the speed-ups and significantly positive after them, so this section measures both quantities directly.

\paragraph{Two budget conventions.}
Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} shows the cost per evaluation of each configuration and the number of evaluations actually completed within the same budget cap. The cost spans about two orders of magnitude: cheaper configurations complete more evaluations, and the open loop completes the most evaluations while using only about one fifth of the wall-clock time.
Comparing at an equal number of generations lets the more expensive configuration use more compute; aligning wall-clock time lets the cheaper configuration complete more evaluations. We therefore report both conventions.

\paragraph{The cost law.}
Reservation-table-aware dispatching raises the number of routing calls per transport task from $2$ to at most $2N_A$, so its cost grows linearly with fleet size.
The lower bound used by the pruning is tighter when contention is weaker, so more candidates are skipped; the larger the fleet and the stronger the contention, the smaller the cut.
The equivalence self-check of Section~\ref{sec:speedup} agrees with this direction: among its six instances, the highest cut rate in routing calls is on the least congested one (\RouteCallCutHi{}) and the lowest on the one with the largest fleet (\RouteCallCutLo{}); the pruning weakens but does not fail.
Together, the two trends \emph{predict} that when the fleet is large enough and contention strong enough, the growing trial-query cost will offset the gain. In Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion}, the sign alternates across the four levels of the vehicle-to-arm ratio and none of the four instances is significant, so this upper bound is not confirmed.
The cost law itself (cost growing linearly with $N_A$, pruning effect falling with $N_A$) is measured directly; the upper bound derived from it has not yet been verified.

\paragraph{Switching the two speed-ups on and off.}
The speed-ups and the dispatching rule were introduced one after the other, so setting the two batches side by side cannot rule out the effect of differences in instances and baselines. We therefore run a separate control that changes only the speed-ups: same instances, same seeds, same budget, with pruning and winner-path reuse switched on or off.

Proposition~\ref{prop:speedup_equiv} guarantees that the two speed-ups \emph{do not change the selected vehicle or the timetable}. On each of six instances we take six random chromosomes; with the speed-ups on and off, the makespans and dispatch sequences are \emph{identical bit for bit} in all $36$ cases, without exception, and routing calls fall by \RouteCallCut{} in total. Since the output is identical, turning the speed-ups off only makes each evaluation slower, and any makespan difference under equal wall-clock time comes \emph{only} from the compute saved.

Table~\ref{tab:prune}: going from off$\to$on lowers makespan by a further \GainPrune{} (\WinPrune{} wins / \LosePrune{} losses, $p\PPrune$); the number of evaluations within the same budget is \EvalRatioPrune{} times as large, and a single evaluation runs \SpeedupProbe{} times faster.
This control links the two batches: the same rule gives \GainProbePre{} on the matrix batch before the speed-ups ($0$ of the $\MatrixCells$ instances significant) and is significantly positive on the ladder batch after them, while the speed-ups themselves change no selection.

\subsection{Degenerate comparison on public benchmarks}
\label{sec:external}

All preceding sections compare different configurations of the same implementation on self-generated instances. This excludes implementation differences among configurations but cannot calibrate the absolute level of the implementation: even if the upper-level search is weak, the differences among configurations remain credible, whereas the absolute makespan reflects only the level of the implementation. This section calibrates the upper-level search on public benchmarks.

\paragraph{What is compared.}
Once the exclusivity constraint, Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl}, is removed, our model degenerates exactly to the standard FJSP\_PT (Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_III}), which is the \emph{same mathematical problem} as the public benchmarks. We additionally set $\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}=0$ to match the makespan definition of these instances; this is a model parameter (Table~\ref{tab:notation}), not the removal of a further constraint.
After degeneration there is no corridor contention and neither mechanism comes into play, so what is tested is the \emph{upper-level search}, not our framework. The results are reported in a table separate from the self-generated instances, so that the two conclusions do not share one table.

\paragraph{Instances and protocol.}
We take the \PubNInst{} instances compiled by Homayouni and Fontes~\cite{homayouni2021production} (originating from Fattahi et al.~\cite{fattahi2007mathematical}; $2$--$12$ jobs, $4$--$48$ operations, $2$--$8$ machines) and, following the setting of Lim and Moon~\cite{lim2023twophase}, use two vehicles with no return of finished products. Travel times are taken \emph{as published} from the released matrices, without shortest-path synthesis: Section~\ref{sec:instances} has noted that \PubNLayoutBad{} of these matrices are not the shortest-path closure of any graph, so synthesis would alter the instances.
After degeneration the road network $G$ enters the model only through $t^{*}$, so the published matrix can be used directly as $t^{*}$ without requiring a consistent $G$ to exist. Contention requires $G$ itself, which these instances do not provide, so they can be used \emph{only} in the degenerate setting.
For two small instances (\textsc{sfjst}01, \textsc{sfjst}03), the published optima were verified digit by digit by hand, confirming that neither includes a return trip.
Reference values: \PubNOpt{} instances have a MILP-proven optimum~\cite{homayouni2021production}, and for the remaining $4$ we use the best published values~\cite{lim2023twophase}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:external} covers \PubNInst{} instances $\times$ \NSeeds{} seeds. All $200$ runs pass three checks: no solution falls below the composite lower bound, no validation fails, and on the \PubNOpt{} instances with a proven optimum ($160$ runs) no run falls below that value. Because the degenerate setting and the literature pose the same problem, this verifies that the conversion and the conventions are correct.
On $\PubNMatch{}/\PubNInst{}$ instances the best solution is \emph{identical digit for digit} to the reference value, including all $10$ small instances (\textsc{sfjst}01--10); the mean gap over the \PubNInst{} instances is \PubGapMean{} (median \PubGapMed{}).
The gap rises with size, but not monotonically from one instance to the next: \textsc{mfjst}03 and \textsc{mfjst}06 both do better than their respective smaller neighbours. The four largest instances ($32$--$48$ operations) average \PubGapBig{}, with a worst case of \PubGapMax{}; none of the $16$ instances with at most $24$ operations exceeds $5.45\%$.

\paragraph{The gap stems mainly from search capability, not from an insufficient budget.}
Among the \PubNOpt{} proven optima in the literature, the MILP on the largest instance took nearly ten hours (\textsc{mfjst}06, $34\,796$ seconds), whereas each of our runs takes only a few seconds. We therefore raised the budget (generation cap five times, early-stopping threshold $3.3$ times, measured wall-clock time increased to \PubBudgetRatio{} times) and re-ran the $10$ \textsc{mfjst} instances in the last ten rows of Table~\ref{tab:external}---which is where the gaps concentrate.
The mean gap of these ten instances falls from \PubGapLoBudget{} to \PubGapHiBudget{} (the base contains only these ten, a different basis from the \PubGapMean{} over the \PubNInst{} instances above); that is, \PubBudgetRatio{} times the computation yields an improvement of only $\PubBudgetGainPP$ percentage points. \PubBudgetNoGain{} of them show no decrease, and the instance with the largest gap falls from $12.93\%$ to $12.85\%$.
Under both budgets, \emph{none} of the $300$ runs reaches the generation cap; all are terminated by early stopping, so the larger budget did not lengthen the search. The gap therefore mainly reflects the capability limit of the upper-level search. Narrowing it on large instances is item~(3) of Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.

\paragraph{Sources of the gap between the composite lower bound and the best solution.}
The median gap between the composite lower bound and the best solution found is \LBGapMed{}. This gap has two components: the amount by which the bound lies below the true optimum, and the amount by which the search solution lies above it. On the self-generated instances the optimum is unknown, so the two components cannot be separated.
On the public instances they can. We take only the \PubNOpt{} instances with a proven optimum: the reference of the other $4$ is a best published value, the true optimum can only be smaller, and using it as the denominator would overestimate ``how far the bound lies below the optimum''.
On these \PubNOpt{} instances, the same composite lower bound lies a median \PubLBSlack{} below the proven optimum and a median \PubLBTotal{} below our best solution; taking the difference instance by instance, the search solution lies a median $\PubLBResidMed$ percentage points above the optimum (most instances hit the optimum), and at most $\PubLBResidMax$ percentage points.
Hence, on these \emph{contention-free} instances, almost all of the gap between the bound and the best solution comes from slack in the bound, not from the search failing to find the optimum.
This proportion cannot be transferred directly to the self-generated instances. The composite lower bound is constructed without corridor contention; on the self-generated instances the true optimum can only rise or stay the same because of contention while the bound does not change, so the distance between the bound and the true optimum is no smaller than on the public instances.
What can be established is that the \LBGapMed{} on the self-generated instances \emph{cannot} be read as a search gap, because the same bound already lies \PubLBSlack{} below the optimum without contention. The distance between the search and the optimum on the self-generated instances remains unknown (Limitation~(3) in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).



\section{Conclusion}

This paper studies scheduling problems in which flexible assignment and conflict-free AGV routing must be decided together, focusing on shops where transport takes longer than processing and congestion concentrates on a few shared corridors: in such shops, when the corridor to a fast arm is congested, a slower robotic arm with better traffic conditions may be the better assignment. Our bilevel framework lets information from lower-level routing enter decisions through two paths, evaluation and dispatching, and measures the gain and cost of each separately under the same wall-clock budget. The main conclusions are as follows.


\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:contributions}

\textbf{(1) Instance families with controllable contention intensity.}
Three families of controlled instances, varying layout, vehicle-to-arm ratio, and flexibility, allow contention intensity to be varied on its own. On them, the closed-loop gain broadly rises with contention intensity, and on the most congested instance it is about two and a half times that on the least congested one, but the relationship is not monotonic (Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion}).

\textbf{(2) A closed-loop bilevel framework with routing-in-the-loop evaluation.}
The upper level searches assignment and sequencing with a memetic algorithm, and the lower level performs conflict-free routing by time-window reservation; the fitness of each candidate solution is the makespan after the lower level has completed all transports.
Proposition~\ref{prop:decodable} and Lemma~\ref{lem:label} guarantee that every chromosome is decodable and that the evaluation is consistent with the conflict model of Section~\ref{sec:Chapter_III}, with no repair operator needed.
Relative to the open loop, the closed loop reduces makespan by $\GainLoopRule$ ($p\PLoopRule$) under rule-based dispatching and by $\GainLoopProbe$ ($p\PLoopProbe$) under reservation-table-aware dispatching.

\textbf{(3) Reservation-table-aware dispatching and two speed-ups that do not change the output.}
Before a vehicle is selected, a trial query on the reservation table is run for both transport legs, and the earliest feasible delivery time replaces the unimpeded shortest-path estimate; within the closed loop this reduces makespan by a further $\GainProbeLoop$ ($p\PProbeLoop$).
The rule raises the cost per evaluation to \CostMultBefore{} times that of rule-based dispatching, enough to offset its gain under the same wall-clock budget. Admissible lower-bound pruning and winner-path reuse change neither the selected vehicle nor the timetable (Proposition~\ref{prop:speedup_equiv}, also confirmed by a bitwise self-check on six instances); they cut router calls by \RouteCallCut{} and lower the cost multiple to \CostMultAfter{}.

\textbf{(4) The two paths differ in magnitude, each is effective, and neither can substitute for the other.}
The closed loop remains significant after Holm correction on \CellsSigLoopAdj{} of the $\NCases$ instances; reservation-table-aware dispatching is significant under both the open and the closed loop only when the instances are pooled for testing ($\GainProbeOpen$, $p\PProbeOpen$; $\GainProbeLoop$, $p\PProbeLoop$, $95\%$ interval \CIProbeLoop{}).
The end-to-end gain $\GainEndToEnd$ is slightly below the prediction under independent composition, $\GainIndepProduct$; the two mechanisms do not amplify each other.
The ``schedule first, then resolve conflicts'' route can obtain only the open-loop dispatching gain of $\GainProbeOpen$; because its first-stage search does not count yielding, the reduction corresponding to closed-loop evaluation ($\GainLoopRule$) is unavailable to it.


\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{(1) Scope of the instance families, and why self-generated instances are used.}
The main experiment fixes the size and varies only the structure (Section~\ref{sec:features}), so we draw no conclusion about how the mechanisms vary with size. Heterogeneity $H$ is fixed at $0.3$; the scan over four $H$ levels comes from the smaller, independent instances of Section~\ref{sec:whichcongestion}, so ``the gain does not rise with $H$'' holds only at \emph{that} size.
The layout family combines two topologies: the three spine instances vary only network capacity, whereas the two grid instances vary topology and distance together (the farthest leg rises from \LegDumbbell{} to \LegGrid{}, Section~\ref{sec:instances}). Moreover, the contention intensity used to characterize this family is a one-dimensional measure that does not separate the part of yielding that reassignment can bypass.
Hence only the differences among the three spine instances can be attributed to capacity alone, and the five instances should not be joined into one curve by contention intensity; these two points are also possible reasons why the gain and contention intensity are not in strictly the same order.
Self-generated instances are used because this direction has no public benchmark with an explicit road network (Section~\ref{sec:instances}).

\textbf{(2) The makespan gain from dispatching is positive on average, but the class of instances on which it appears stably cannot yet be determined.}
$\NSeeds$ seeds per instance suffice to resolve the closed-loop effect (significant on $\CellsSigLoopAdj$ of the $\NCases$ instances after Holm correction). The effect of reservation-table-aware dispatching is only a few percentage points, of the same order as the dispersion across seeds: of the $\NCases$ instances, $\CellsSigProbe$ are significant before correction and $\CellsSigProbeAdj$ after.
The gain can therefore be confirmed as positive only on the $\NPairs$ pooled pairs ($95\%$ interval \CIProbeLoop{}, whose lower end is less than one percentage point from zero).
Raising the number of seeds by about an order of magnitude would be needed to determine the class of instances on which it appears and to test the fleet-size upper bound predicted by the cost law of Section~\ref{sec:costlaw}.

\textbf{(3) The upper-level search has been calibrated on contention-free public benchmarks; with corridor contention, the distance of solutions from the optimum cannot yet be determined.}
All configurations share one implementation, and what is reported are the differences among configurations; the strength of the upper-level search is common to all configurations and does not affect those differences. The degenerate comparison of Section~\ref{sec:external} matches the literature digit for digit on $\PubNMatch{}/\PubNInst{}$ instances, and all $10$ small instances reach the proven optimum. The gap rises with size: the four largest instances average \PubGapBig{}, and \PubBudgetRatio{} times the computation improves it by only $\PubBudgetGainPP$ percentage points, so the gap reflects a capability limit rather than an insufficient budget.
On the \PubNOpt{} public instances with a proven optimum, the search solution is at most $\PubLBResidMax$ percentage points from the optimum, and the bound gap comes almost entirely from slack in the bound. On the self-generated instances the composite lower bound contains no contention, so its median gap \LBGapMed{} to the best solution cannot be read as a search gap; we draw no quantitative conclusion about this distance.
The public instances contain no corridor contention, and lower-level routing with the reservation table has no proven optimum to compare against; the much smaller effect of a few percentage points (such as \GainProbeLoop{}) may be of the same order as the distance of the implementation from the optimum.

\textbf{(4) The surcharge and yield-record-guided local search did not further reduce makespan; if their evaluation cost is reduced on its own, they must be remeasured.}
Both were assessed in the same implementation under the same wall-clock budget, so what was measured is the trade-off between cost per evaluation and number of evaluations: when all configurations are sped up by the same factor, that trade-off and the sign of the conclusion stay unchanged; only reducing the cost of one interface on its own could change the sign, as the dispatching speed-ups of Section~\ref{sec:speedup} illustrate.
Hence, if neighbourhood evaluation is changed to re-solve only the perturbed part, they must be remeasured (item~(4) of Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

\textbf{(5) Dynamic disruptions such as robotic-arm and vehicle failures are not considered.}
Assumption A7 already excludes robotic-arm and vehicle failures and dynamic job arrivals from the model. The reported results are limited to the static setting in which the job set and processing times are known over the planning horizon and equipment does not fail. Under failures or online arrivals, the value of a mechanism also includes how fast it repairs a schedule, not only the quality of the schedule it constructs; this aspect is not measured here (item~(5) of Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future_work}

\textbf{(1) Remeasure on larger explicit road networks, and separate contention from chain substitutability.}
This corresponds to Limitation~(1). Since the main experiment fixes the size, the first step is to test on larger explicit corridor networks whether the closed-loop and dispatching gains keep the same magnitude. In layout design, ``whether congestion can be bypassed by reassignment'' and ``whether the critical chain has only one dominant bottleneck'' should be treated as two independent factors; the current contention intensity is a one-dimensional measure that does not separate congestion bypassable by reassignment, so a new measure, first validated on instances whose answers are known, is needed.

\textbf{(2) Raise the number of seeds to determine the class of instances on which the dispatching gain appears stably.}
This corresponds to Limitation~(2). The makespan gain from dispatching has been confirmed to be positive on average. Raising the number of seeds by about an order of magnitude and sampling the vehicle-to-arm ratio at denser levels would determine the class of instances on which it appears stably and test the cost law: whether there is a fleet size beyond which the growth in trial-query cost offsets the gain.

\textbf{(3) Measure the distance of solutions from the optimum on instances with corridor contention.}
This corresponds to Limitation~(3). The \PubGapBig{} on the large public instances is insensitive to longer search time, the standard deviation across seeds on the four instances reaches \PubCvBig{} of the mean, and all runs are terminated by early stopping, so it is the encoding or the neighbourhood that needs improvement.
At the small end, exact solutions obtained with the logic-based Benders decomposition of van Os and Basten~\cite{vanos2026exact} would separate, under corridor contention, the shares due to bound slack and to insufficient search, and would test whether the surcharge and yield-record-guided local search still fail to reduce makespan under a stronger search.
A stronger search does not affect the differences among configurations, but may help determine the class of instances on which the dispatching gain appears stably (Limitation~(2)).

\textbf{(4) Reduce cost by incremental evaluation, then remeasure yield-record-guided local search.}
This corresponds to Limitation~(4). The decoder state advances in one direction, so when a perturbation acts only on the sequence after some position, earlier events and reservations are unchanged. Re-solving only the perturbed part amounts to reducing the cost per evaluation of this kind of local search on its own, after which it can be remeasured under the same wall-clock budget.

\textbf{(5) Measure the speed of schedule repair under robotic-arm and vehicle failures.}
This corresponds to Limitation~(5). Work on the dynamic flexible job shop has already brought machine failures, rush orders, and job arrivals into rescheduling~\cite{gui2023dynamic,xiong2024dynamic,dauzereperes2024fjsp}.
In that case the value of a mechanism also includes the speed of repairing a schedule, not only the time and quality of constructing one. Whether an evaluator that re-solves from scratch or a method that modifies only a local neighbourhood holds the advantage cannot be carried over directly from conclusions in the static setting.
